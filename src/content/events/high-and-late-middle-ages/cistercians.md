dating: |
  The Exordium Parvum dates the departure from Molesme to 1098 [[cite:ep-1]]. William of Saint-Thierry puts Bernard's entry at Cîteaux in 1113, the fifteenth year of the house, when Bernard was about twenty-two or twenty-three (the manuscripts differ) [[cite:vp-entry]]. He gives no year for the founding of Clairvaux [[cite:vp-clairvaux]]. The editorial commentary printed with the Life in Migne's Patrologia Latina (1855) quotes a tablet that hung at Bernard's tomb, which said that he was sent to Clairvaux in June 1115, reports that an earlier scholar argued for June 1114, and prefers 1115 [[cite:vp-commentary]]. The timeline follows that year.

  The date of the early Cistercian documents themselves is debated. Constance H. Berman, *The Cistercian Evolution: The Invention of a Religious Order in Twelfth-Century Europe* (2000), argued that the Exordium Parvum and the Charter of Charity reached their surviving form only in the 1160s and 1170s. They are cited here as the Order's own account of its beginnings.

In 1098, according to the Cistercians' own account of their origins, [[person:robert-of-molesme]] and some of his monks went to the papal legate, Archbishop Hugh of Lyon, and declared that they wished to keep the [[event:rule-of-benedict|Rule of Benedict]] more strictly than it was kept in their abbey. With his authority twenty-one monks left Molesme for [[place:citeaux]], a wooded and unvisited place in the diocese of Chalon, cleared it and began a wooden monastery, which the duke of Burgundy completed for them. Their documents call it the New Monastery [[cite:ep-1]]. The monks of Molesme soon appealed to Pope [[person:urban-ii]]; the legate sent Robert back, and the prior, Alberic, became abbot [[cite:ep-5]].

The same account lists what the new community decided. It gave up whatever in dress and diet went beyond the Rule. It refused revenues such as churches, tithes, manors and serfs, on the ground that Benedict had none and that monks should live from their own land and work. To make that possible it took in bearded lay brothers, who were not monks, to run its outlying farms, and it resolved to build only in places far from towns [[cite:ep-15]]. Under Alberic's successor, the Englishman [[person:stephen-harding]], the church was stripped to wooden crosses, an iron candlestick and vestments of plain cloth. Recruits were few, and the monks feared they would have no successors [[cite:ep-16]].

Then, the account says, thirty men entered the novitiate together [[cite:ep-16]]. William of Saint-Thierry, in his Life of [[person:bernard-of-clairvaux]], says that Bernard entered Cîteaux in 1113 with more than thirty companions [[cite:vp-entry]]. Some time later Stephen sent out a group of monks to found [[place:clairvaux]], in the diocese of Langres, and set Bernard over them as abbot, to their surprise, since he was young and in poor health. The valley, William says, had been called the Valley of Wormwood and was a haunt of robbers; the monks at first ate bread of barley, millet and vetch and cooked beech leaves [[cite:vp-clairvaux]].

New foundations followed quickly: twelve houses within eight years [[cite:ep-18]]. The Charter of Charity, drawn up by Stephen and his monks [[cite:cc-prologue]], bound them together. The mother house takes no payment from a daughter house. All keep the Rule as it is kept at the New Monastery, with the same books and customs. Each abbot visits his daughter houses once a year, and all the abbots meet once a year at Cîteaux [[cite:cc]].

Bernard wrote as well as governed. His treatise *On Loving God*, written as abbot for a Roman cardinal, answers that God is to be loved for his own sake and without measure, and traces four degrees by which love grows [[cite:love-dedication]] [[cite:love-1]] [[cite:love-15]]. When he died in 1153, Geoffrey of Auxerre, a monk of Clairvaux, called him the father of more than a hundred and sixty monasteries [[cite:vp-death]].
