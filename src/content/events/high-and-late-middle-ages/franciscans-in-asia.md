  The pope's two letters are dated at Lyon on 5 and 13 March in his second year, 1245 [[cite:dei-patris]] [[cite:cum-non-solum]]. John of Plano Carpini gives the days of his stay at the khan's court by the church calendar, without the year [[cite:carpini-court]]; the Persian text of the khan's reply is dated to the last days of the month Jumada II of the year 644, which its editor, Paul Pelliot, converts to 3–11 November 1246 [[cite:guyuk]]. William of Rubruck dates his sailing to 7 May 1253 [[cite:rubruck-sailing]], and John of Montecorvino dates his letters from Khanbaliq to 8 January 1305 and to Quinquagesima Sunday in February 1306 [[cite:montecorvino-1]] [[cite:montecorvino-2]].

  The closing year is that of the creation of the archbishopric. Henry Yule, *Cathay and the Way Thither* (second edition, revised by Henri Cordier, vol. 3, 1914), pp. 9–10, places it in the spring of 1307, noting that only an undated fragment of the bull survives and that the letter naming one of the suffragan bishops is dated 1 May 1307 [[cite:see]]. Andrew of Perugia puts the consecration itself at Khanbaliq in 1308, "as well as I can reckon" [[cite:andrew]].

In March 1245 [[person:innocent-iv]], then at [[place:lyon]], addressed two letters to the king and people of the Tartars. The first sets out the Christian faith: the Father sent his Son, who was born of a virgin, died on the cross, rose, and left on earth a vicar holding the keys of the kingdom of heaven. It commends the Franciscan Laurence of Portugal and his companions [[cite:dei-patris]]. The second says that the pope is astonished to hear that they have invaded many countries, Christian and other, and laid them waste, sparing neither sex nor age. It urges them to stop, above all to stop persecuting Christians, and to say through his envoys what they intend [[cite:cum-non-solum]].

[[person:john-of-plano-carpini]], a Franciscan, writes that he told the first Mongol chiefs he met what the pope asked of them: to become Christians, to cease the slaughter and to write what they intended [[cite:carpini-message]]. He reached the camp of Güyük on 22 July and saw him enthroned as emperor on 24 August [[cite:carpini-court]]. Christians of the khan's household told him they believed that Güyük was about to become a Christian; he kept Christian clerks, John writes, and a chapel stood before his tent [[cite:carpini-chapel]]. On 11 November the khan's secretaries went through the reply with the friars word by word while they put it into Latin, and it was written out "in Saracenic" as well [[cite:carpini-letter]]. A Persian text of the letter, found in the Vatican archives in 1920 [[cite:guyuk-found]], says that the request that the khan be baptised has not been understood; that God destroyed the peoples the pope speaks of, because they disobeyed God's order and killed Mongol envoys; and that the pope must come in person with the kings to offer service and homage. If they do not, the Mongols will know them as enemies [[cite:guyuk]].

[[person:william-of-rubruck]], also a Franciscan, sailed into the Black Sea in May 1253 [[cite:rubruck-sailing]]. He had preached in Saint Sophia that he was no one's envoy, but was going among unbelievers as the rule of his order directed [[cite:rubruck-start]]. He reached the camp of the khan Möngke, his "Mangu Chan", on 27 December [[cite:rubruck-arrival]]. In [[place:karakorum]], he writes, there were twelve temples of idols, two mosques and one Christian church [[cite:rubruck-city]]. On the eve of Pentecost 1254, by the khan's order and before three umpires, he argued with one of those he calls Tuins, or idolaters; at the end, he writes, nobody said "I believe; I want to become a Christian" [[cite:rubruck-debate]]. Next day Möngke told him that as God gave the hand different fingers, so he gave men different ways [[cite:rubruck-audience]]. William left with a letter from the khan to King Louis of France, which told the king to send envoys if he would obey [[cite:rubruck-letter]]; he had baptised, he records, six people [[cite:rubruck-leave]].

[[person:john-of-montecorvino]] says that he left Tabriz in 1291, spent thirteen months in India and went on to Cathay with a letter from the pope to the great khan. Writing from [[place:beijing|Khanbaliq]] in January 1305, he reports a church with a bell-tower, some six thousand people baptised by his own estimate, and a choir of boys whom he had bought, baptised and taught. The Nestorians, he says, had accused him for five years of being a spy and an impostor [[cite:montecorvino-1]]. Andrew of Perugia, a bishop sent out by the Apostolic See, writes that he reached the city in 1308, by his reckoning, and that John was consecrated archbishop there [[cite:andrew]].

William met priests of the Church of the East at Möngke's court, and John found them powerful in Cathay; both call them Nestorians [[cite:rubruck-nestorians]] [[cite:montecorvino-1]]. For that church's coming to China in the seventh century see [[event:alopen-in-china|the mission of Aluoben]].
