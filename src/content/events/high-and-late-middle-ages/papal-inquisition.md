The Fourth Lateran Council had made the search for heretics the duty of each bishop, who was to visit suspect parishes and put the neighbours on oath [[cite:lateran]].

In 1231 [[person:gregory-ix]] issued statutes of his own. They excommunicate Cathars, Patarenes, the Poor of Lyon and other named groups. Those the church condemns are to be left to the secular court for "the punishment due". Those who are caught and then return to do penance are to be imprisoned for life; the version in the church's law book reads, of those who will not return [[cite:statutes]] [[cite:decretals]]. Supporters lose their legal rights, suspects who do not clear themselves within a year are condemned as heretics, no appeal is heard, and no advocate or notary may act for them. Laymen may not dispute about the faith. Everyone who knows of heretics or secret meetings must report them. Their children to the second generation are barred from church office [[cite:statutes]].

In November 1231 Gregory wrote to the Dominican prior of Regensburg and a fellow friar. They were to assemble clergy and people and preach, then with chosen helpers "inquire diligently" after heretics and those of ill repute. Any found guilty who would not obey the church absolutely were to be dealt with under the new statutes. Those who abjured were to be absolved and given the customary penance, with care taken against pretended conversions. The letter gives them power to censure anyone who resists and adds that two of them may act if not all can be present [[cite:ille]].

William Pelhisson, a friar of the convent at [[place:toulouse]], describes the work there. The pope made two Dominicans inquisitors for the dioceses of Toulouse and Cahors [[cite:appointed]]. They offered a time of grace, in which those who confessed fully were promised that they would not be imprisoned, exiled or stripped of their goods [[cite:grace]]. They summoned suspects and heard witnesses. One man, John Textor, protested that he was a married, meat-eating Christian. Pelhisson, who calls him accursed, says that he was condemned in the friars' cloister, that in the bishop's prison he then declared himself of one belief with heretics held there, and that he refused to recant and was burned with them. At Cahors the friars condemned dead persons, whose bodies were dragged through the town and burned [[cite:textor]]. In November 1235 the consuls drove the friars out of Toulouse. One of them went to the pope at [[place:rome]], and Gregory had the count restore them [[cite:expelled]].

In 1252 Innocent IV ordered the city governments of northern Italy to hand arrested heretics to the bishop or the inquisitors within fifteen days, to apply the laws against heretics to those condemned, and to force those arrested, stopping short of loss of limb or danger of death, to confess their errors and name others, as thieves are forced to name accomplices [[cite:extirpanda]].
