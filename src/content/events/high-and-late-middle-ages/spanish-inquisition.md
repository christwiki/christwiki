  The bull is dated at Rome on 1 November 1478 [[cite:bull-1478]]. Ferdinand and Isabella acted on it on 27 September 1480, when they commissioned two Dominicans as inquisitors [[cite:commission]], and Bernáldez, who calls these two the first inquisitors, puts the start of their work at Seville at the beginning of 1481 [[cite:seville]]. The edict of expulsion is dated at Granada on 31 March 1492 [[cite:edict]].

  The numbers are disputed and no complete records survive. For the tribunal of Seville from 1481 to 1488 Bernáldez gives more than 700 burned and more than 5,000 reconciled [[cite:seville]]; for the Jews of Castile and Aragon at the time of the edict he reports 35,000 or 36,000 households, more than 160,000 people [[cite:exiles]]. Henry Charles Lea, *A History of the Inquisition of Spain*, vol. 4 (1907), pp. 517–519, sets out how widely later estimates of those burned differ: one writer allowed fewer than 400 for the whole history of the tribunal and another 2,000 down to 1504, while Juan Antonio Llorente calculated 8,800 by the end of Torquemada's office, by a method that Lea calls absurd. The queen's secretary Hernando del Pulgar, as Lea reports him, put the burned in all Spain at 2,000 and those reconciled under edicts of grace at 15,000. On the exiles Lea, vol. 1 (1906), p. 142, lists estimates from Bernáldez's 160,000 to 800,000 in later Spanish writers, cites Isidore Loeb's reckoning of 1887 (165,000 emigrants, 50,000 baptised, 20,000 dead), and judges that all such figures are "more or less a matter of guess-work". Estimates made since Lea wrote are not given here, because this entry could not confirm them.

On 1 November 1478 [[person:sixtus-iv]] answered a petition from [[person:ferdinand-ii-of-aragon]] and [[person:isabella-i-of-castile]], king and queen of Castile and León. They had told him that in their kingdoms many who had been baptised, without having been strictly forced to it, were returning to the rites of the Jews while passing as Christians. The pope allowed them to choose, for each city or diocese, three bishops or other priests over forty years old and trained in theology or canon law. These men were to have against such people the jurisdiction that bishops and inquisitors have by law. The king and queen could remove and replace them [[cite:bull-1478]].

Andrés Bernáldez, parish priest of Los Palacios, who approved of the work, describes what followed at [[place:seville]]. Two Dominican friars began their inquisition there at the start of 1481. They arrested some of the city's richest and most honoured citizens and burned six men and women at the first burning. Many fled. In eight years, he says, the tribunal burned more than 700 people and reconciled more than 5,000 [[cite:seville]].

Complaints reached Rome. On 29 January 1482 Sixtus wrote to the king and queen that his bull had been drawn up contrary to the decrees of the fathers, its terms having been put to him only in general. The two inquisitors at Seville, it was said, had proceeded without order of law, imprisoned many unjustly, put them to cruel torments, declared them heretics unjustly and taken the goods of those put to death. He left the two in office, ordered them to act together with the bishops, and refused to let the monarchs name inquisitors in their other kingdoms [[cite:brief-jan]]. On 18 April he wrote that, as he had been informed, the inquisition in Aragon, Valencia, Mallorca and Catalonia was being conducted not from zeal for the faith and the salvation of souls but from greed for gain. He ordered that the accused be told the names and testimony of their accusers, be allowed advocates, be held only in the bishops' prisons, and be free to appeal to Rome [[cite:bull-april]]. Ferdinand replied on 13 May that he gave the report no credit, that if such things had been granted he did not intend ever to let them take effect, and that inquisitors must be appointed at his pleasure [[cite:ferdinand]].

In February 1482 the pope himself had named eight more Dominicans as inquisitors in Castile and León, among them [[person:tomas-de-torquemada]] [[cite:brief-feb]]. By November 1484 Torquemada was inquisitor general, and inquisitors and lawyers summoned by him to Seville drew up instructions [[cite:instructions-preamble]]. On arriving in a town the inquisitors were to preach and announce thirty or forty days of grace. Those who came in that time and told all they knew of themselves and of others would escape death, perpetual prison and the loss of their goods [[cite:instructions-grace]]. The reconciled would abjure in public and be barred for life from public office and from a list of trades, from wearing silk or gold, from riding and from bearing arms. Money penances were to go to the war against the Moors of [[place:granada]]. Those who confessed after the term lost their property [[cite:instructions-penalties]].

On 31 March 1492, at Granada, the king and queen ordered every Jew to leave their kingdoms by the end of July and never return, on pain of death. The edict gives the reason: some Christians were judaizing, and contact with Jews, who taught them their law, was much to blame. Separate quarters, twelve years of inquisition and the removal of the Jews from Andalusia had not been enough. They might sell their property and take their goods, but no gold, silver or coin [[cite:edict]]. Bernáldez describes their going. They sold a house for a donkey and a vineyard for a piece of cloth, he writes, and took the roads, old and young, on foot and on asses; there was no Christian who did not feel sorry for them. They were urged to accept baptism all along the way, and very few did [[cite:exiles]].
