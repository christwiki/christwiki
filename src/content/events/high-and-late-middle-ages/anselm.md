  The end date is better fixed. Anselm's own preface says that Cur Deus Homo was begun in England and finished in exile [[cite:cdh-preface]]. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle puts his departure from England in the autumn of 1097 [[cite:asc-1097]], and Eadmer, who was with him, says that he completed the book in a hilltop village to which he had withdrawn from the summer heat of Rome [[cite:eadmer-cdh]]. That summer was 1098.

[[person:anselm-of-canterbury]] became a monk at [[place:bec]] in Normandy in his twenty-seventh year and, three years later, its prior, according to his companion and biographer Eadmer [[cite:eadmer-bec]]. There he wrote a meditation on the grounds of faith, the Monologion, and then looked for something shorter: one argument that would be enough by itself to show that God exists and is what Christians believe him to be [[cite:preface]] [[cite:eadmer-proslogion]]. He says that the search wore him out and that the argument came to him when he was trying to put it out of his mind [[cite:preface]]. He called the little book that resulted *Faith Seeking Understanding*, and later *Proslogion* [[cite:preface]]. Eadmer adds that the first draft, written on wax tablets, was lost, and the second found broken on the floor [[cite:eadmer-proslogion]].

The book is a prayer. "I do not seek to understand that I may believe," Anselm writes, "but I believe in order to understand" [[cite:pros-1]]. God, he says, is believed to be "a being than which nothing greater can be conceived". Even the fool of the psalm, who says in his heart that there is no God [[bible:Ps 14:1]], understands those words, so that such a being exists at least in his understanding. But a being that existed in the understanding alone would be less than one that also existed in reality. So that than which nothing greater can be conceived must exist in reality [[cite:pros-2]], and cannot even be conceived not to exist [[cite:pros-3]].

A monk, Gaunilo, answered on the fool's behalf. By the same reasoning, he wrote, one could prove the existence of a lost island richer than every other land [[cite:gaunilo]]. Anselm replied that the reasoning holds for nothing except that than which a greater cannot be conceived [[cite:reply]], and asked that the objection and his answer be copied with the book from then on [[cite:eadmer-proslogion]].

In 1093 King William II gave Anselm, by then abbot of Bec, the archbishopric of [[place:canterbury]] [[cite:asc-1093]]. He left England in 1097, judging that little was done there according to right [[cite:asc-1097]]. In exile he finished *Cur Deus Homo*, "Why God Became Man", a dialogue that sets Christ aside, as if nothing were known of him, and asks by reason alone why God should have become man and died [[cite:cdh-preface]] [[cite:cdh-1-1]]. To sin, Anselm argues, is to withhold from God the honour that is his due, and the sinner owes satisfaction for it [[cite:cdh-1-11]]. It would not be fitting for God simply to remit the debt by mercy [[cite:cdh-1-12]]. The satisfaction required is one that none but God can make and none but man ought to make; therefore it must be made by one who is both God and man [[cite:cdh-2-6]].

Anselm came back to England in 1100 and died in 1109 [[cite:asc-1100]] [[cite:asc-1109]]. Both books follow the procedure their openings announce: not to reach faith by reasoning, but to understand by reasoning what is already believed [[cite:pros-1]] [[cite:cdh-1-1]]. The schools of the following century are the subject of [[event:rise-of-the-universities]].
