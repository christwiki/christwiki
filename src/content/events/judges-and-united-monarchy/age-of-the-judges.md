  Judges gives no date that can be tied to an outside record. It counts years of oppression and of peace, several of them round forties [[bible:Judg 3:11; 5:31; 8:28; 13:1]]. Added end to end, its figures come to 410 years [[bible:Judg 3:8, 11, 14, 30; 4:3; 5:31; 6:1; 8:28; 9:22; 10:2-3, 8; 12:7-14; 13:1; 15:20]]. Kings counts 480 years from the exodus to Solomon's fourth year [[bible:1 Kings 6:1]], and Jephthah speaks of 300 years since Israel settled east of the Jordan [[bible:Judg 11:26]].

  Two chronologies are in use. Taking the 480 years as an exact figure puts the exodus about 1446 BC and the first judges in the fourteenth century BC, from about 1375 BC; so Andrew E. Steinmann, "The Mysterious Numbers of the Book of Judges", *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 48 (2005), pp. 491–500, and *From Abraham to Paul: A Biblical Chronology* (2011). Steinmann notes that even then the 410 years do not fit between the conquest and David, and he has some of the later judges overlap. Many historians instead take the 480 years as a round or schematic number and the judges as regional leaders whose terms overlapped, and place the period between about 1200 and 1050 BC, after the Egyptian inscription of about 1208 BC that names Israel in Canaan ([[event:merneptah-stele]]); so K. A. Kitchen, *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* (2003), who puts the exodus in the thirteenth century BC.

  The timeline shows the shorter range. That differs from the entries before this one: [[event:the-exodus]] and [[event:covenant-renewal-at-shechem]] are placed at the earlier of their two dates, about 1446 and about 1380 BC, and on that reckoning the judges begin about 175 years before the first year shown here. The period ends with the accession of Saul, which is itself only approximately dated ([[event:saul-anointed-king]]). No inscription from the period can be securely connected with any of the judges.

The book of Judges begins after the death of [[person:joshua]]. A new generation arose, it says, that did not know the Lord or what he had done for Israel. The people served the Baals and were handed over to their enemies; when they groaned under oppression the Lord raised up judges who rescued them; and when each judge died they turned back to other gods [[bible:Judg 2:8-19]]. The stories that follow are set inside this pattern.

[[person:deborah]], a prophetess who sat as judge in the hill country of Ephraim, summoned Barak to gather ten thousand men of Naphtali and Zebulun on [[place:mount-tabor]]. They faced Sisera, the commander of the Canaanite king Jabin of Hazor, who had nine hundred iron chariots [[bible:Judg 4:2-10]]. Barak went down from the mountain and Sisera's army was destroyed. Sisera fled on foot and was killed in her tent by Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite [[bible:Judg 4:14-22]]. The Song of Deborah tells of the same victory in verse and names the tribes that fought and those that stayed at home [[bible:Judg 5:12-23]].

[[person:gideon]] of Manasseh was called while he threshed wheat in a winepress to hide it from Midianite raiders [[bible:Judg 6:11-16]]. He pulled down his father's altar of Baal by night [[bible:Judg 6:25-32]]. On the Lord's instruction he cut his force to three hundred men, and with trumpets, jars and torches threw the Midianite camp into panic in the dark [[bible:Judg 7:2-8, 16-22]]. When the Israelites asked him and his sons to rule them he refused: "Yahweh shall rule over you" [[bible:Judg 8:22-23]]. But the ephod he made from the plunder became an object of worship in his town [[bible:Judg 8:24-27]].

[[person:samson]], of the tribe of Dan, was dedicated as a Nazirite before his birth, in years when the Philistines ruled Israel [[bible:Judg 13:1-5]]. Alone, he killed a thousand of them at Lehi with the jawbone of a donkey [[bible:Judg 15:14-16]]. Delilah learned that his strength lay in his uncut hair and betrayed him. The Philistines blinded him and set him to grind grain in the prison at [[place:gaza]], and he died pulling down the house in which their rulers were celebrating a sacrifice to their god Dagon [[bible:Judg 16:15-30]].

The last chapters tell of a private shrine with a carved image that the tribe of Dan carried off [[bible:Judg 17:1-6; 18:27-31]] and of a war of the other tribes against Benjamin [[bible:Judg 20:8-14, 43-48]]. The book closes with the line, "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" [[bible:Judg 21:25]]. The books of Samuel carry the story on to Israel's demand for a king ([[event:saul-anointed-king]]). The letter to the Hebrews names Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah among those who acted through faith [[bible:Heb 11:32-34]].
