dating: |
  Josephus says that Herod reigned thirty-seven years from the day the Romans declared him king and thirty-four from the death of his rival Antigonus [[cite:death]] [[cite:war-death]]. He dates the first to the consuls of 40 BC [[cite:appointed]] and the capture of Jerusalem that led to the second to the consuls of 37 BC [[cite:captured]]. Counted inclusively, both figures end in 4 BC. He also sets the death after an eclipse of the moon [[cite:eclipse]] and before a Passover [[cite:passover]]. Astronomical tables show a partial eclipse on the night of 12–13 March 4 BC, about a month before Passover.

  The reigns of Herod's sons are counted from the same year. Archelaus was deposed in his tenth year [[cite:tenth-year]], which Cassius Dio puts under the consuls of AD 6 [[cite:dio]], and Philip died in the twentieth year of Tiberius after ruling thirty-seven years [[cite:philip]]. For these reasons 4 BC is the usual date: see Emil Schürer, *The History of the Jewish People in the Age of Jesus Christ*, revised by G. Vermes and F. Millar, vol. 1 (1973), in the section on Herod the Great. T. D. Barnes, "The Date of Herod's Death", *Journal of Theological Studies* 19 (1968), pp. 204–209, prefers 5 BC and allows 4 BC.

  A minority of scholars count Herod's years differently and connect the death with the total eclipse of 9–10 January 1 BC: W. E. Filmer, "The Chronology of the Reign of Herod the Great", *Journal of Theological Studies* 17 (1966), pp. 283–298, and Andrew E. Steinmann, "When Did Herod the Great Reign?", *Novum Testamentum* 51 (2009), pp. 1–29. The timeline follows the majority.

[[person:josephus]] describes the last months of [[person:herod-the-great]] at length. He writes that the king was about seventy and gravely ill [[cite:illness]]. Two teachers of the law in [[place:jerusalem]], Judas and Matthias, told their students that the golden eagle Herod had set over the great gate of the temple broke the law against images, and a group of young men cut it down in broad daylight. No fewer than forty were arrested with their teachers. Herod had Matthias and his companions burned alive, and Josephus adds that there was an eclipse of the moon that night [[cite:eagle]].

The king sought relief at the hot springs of Callirrhoe beyond the [[place:jordan-river|Jordan]] and then returned to [[place:jericho]] [[cite:illness]]. There, Josephus says, he had leading men from the whole nation shut in the hippodrome and ordered that they be killed at his death, so that the country would mourn; his sister Salome released them instead [[cite:hippodrome]]. He had his son Antipater executed and died five days later [[cite:death]].

His final will named [[person:archelaus]] king, made [[person:herod-antipas]] tetrarch of Galilee and Perea, and gave another son, Philip, the districts north-east of the [[place:sea-of-galilee]]. It was to take effect only when the emperor had confirmed it [[cite:will]]. Archelaus buried his father at Herodium [[cite:funeral]]. He told the crowds in the temple that he would not use the title of king until [[person:augustus]] granted it. When mourners for the executed teachers stoned his soldiers at Passover, he sent in his army, and about three thousand people were killed [[cite:passover]].

Archelaus then sailed for [[place:rome]] [[cite:sailed]]. A delegation of fifty from Judea followed and asked Augustus to end the rule of Herod's family and attach the country to the province of Syria [[cite:embassy]]. Augustus gave Archelaus half the kingdom, namely Judea, Samaria and Idumea, with the title of ethnarch and the promise of kingship if he ruled well. Antipas received Galilee and Perea, and Philip received Batanea, Trachonitis and Auranitis [[cite:division]] [[cite:war-division]].

Matthew sets the childhood of [[person:jesus]] against these events. In his account Joseph, who had fled to Egypt with the child and his mother, returns after Herod's death, is afraid to settle in Judea when he hears that Archelaus rules there, and makes his home at [[place:nazareth]] in Galilee [[bible:Matt 2:19-23]]. The division shaped the setting of the Gospels: Luke names Herod Antipas as tetrarch of Galilee and Philip as tetrarch of Ituraea and Trachonitis when [[person:john-the-baptist]] began to preach [[bible:Luke 3:1]].
