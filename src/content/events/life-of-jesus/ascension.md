dating: |
  Acts says that Jesus appeared to the apostles "over a period of forty days" and was then taken up [[bible:Acts 1:3, 9]]. Counted from the Sunday on which the Gospels place the resurrection, the fortieth day is a Thursday: 18 May if the year was AD 30, 14 May if it was AD 33 (see [[event:crucifixion]] for the two years). The feast of the Ascension is traditionally kept on that Thursday. Forty is also a round number in Scripture [[bible:Exod 24:18; Mark 1:13]], and Luke's Gospel, read by itself, moves from the resurrection to the departure without a break [[bible:Luke 24:36, 50-51]]; the two books have the same author [[bible:Luke 1:1-4; Acts 1:1-2]], who gives the fuller account in the second.

  Apart from one sentence in the longer ending of Mark [[bible:Mark 16:19]], Luke is the only New Testament writer who narrates the event. In the Gospel the words "and was carried up into heaven" are missing from the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus, where a corrector has added them [[cite:sinaiticus]], and the NRSVUE notes that other ancient authorities lack them. The place is "as far as Bethany" in the Gospel and the Mount of Olives in Acts [[bible:Luke 24:50; Acts 1:12]]; Bethany lay on the Mount of Olives [[bible:Mark 11:1]]. The spot shown on the mount goes back to the fourth century [[cite:eusebius]] [[cite:egeria]].

Luke tells of the departure of [[person:jesus]] twice. At the end of his Gospel, Jesus leads the disciples out as far as [[place:bethany]], lifts his hands and blesses them, and while he blesses them he is carried up into heaven. They return to Jerusalem with great joy and are continually in the temple praising God [[bible:Luke 24:50-53]].

The opening of Acts is fuller. For forty days Jesus had shown himself alive to the apostles and spoken about the kingdom of God [[bible:Acts 1:3]]. He told them not to leave Jerusalem but to wait for "the promise of the Father": within a few days they would be baptised in the Holy Spirit [[bible:Acts 1:4-5]]. They asked whether he would now restore the kingdom to Israel. He answered that the times were not theirs to know, and that they would receive power and be his witnesses "in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth" [[bible:Acts 1:6-8]]. Then, as they watched, "he was taken up, and a cloud received him out of their sight". Two men in white told them that this Jesus would come back in the same way as they had seen him go [[bible:Acts 1:9-11]]. They walked back to the city from the [[place:mount-of-olives]], a Sabbath day's journey away [[bible:Acts 1:12]].

The other Gospels do not narrate the scene. Matthew ends in Galilee with the promise "I am with you always" [[bible:Matt 28:20]]. In John the risen Jesus tells [[person:mary-magdalene]], "I am ascending to my Father and your Father" [[bible:John 20:17]]. The longer ending of Mark says in one sentence that he "was received up into heaven, and sat down at the right hand of God" [[bible:Mark 16:19]]; on those verses see [[event:appearances-and-great-commission]].

Elsewhere the New Testament speaks less of a departure than of an enthronement. Peter's speech at Pentecost says that Jesus has been exalted to the right hand of God, and applies to him the psalm "Sit at my right hand" [[bible:Acts 2:33-35; Ps 110:1]]. The same language recurs in the letters [[bible:Eph 1:20; Heb 1:3]].

For Luke the event closes the time of the appearances and opens the time of the church: not many days later comes [[event:pentecost]] [[bible:Acts 1:5; Acts 2:1-4]]. The creed joins it to the expectation of Christ's return: he "ascended into heaven, and sitteth at the Right Hand of the Father. And he shall come again with glory" [[cite:creed]]. By the fourth century Christians marked the place on the Mount of Olives. Eusebius says that the mother of the emperor Constantine built a church on the mount of the ascension [[cite:eusebius]], and the pilgrim Egeria knew a spot there called the Imbomon, "the place whence the Lord ascended into heaven" [[cite:egeria]].
