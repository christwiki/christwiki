dating: |
  The letters carry no year. The letter to Rome is dated in late August [[cite:rom-10]], and nothing in the seven letters names an emperor or a governor. The date rests on Eusebius, who wrote two centuries later and sets Ignatius's episcopate, journey and death in his account of the reign of [[person:trajan]] (AD 98–117) [[cite:eusebius-trajan]] [[cite:eusebius]]. [[person:jerome]], later still, puts the death in Trajan's eleventh year, about AD 108 [[cite:jerome]]. The letters are therefore usually placed around 110. Because the year comes from this later tradition and not from the letters, the date is marked traditional.

  Some historians hold that Eusebius had no firm information and date the letters later. Timothy D. Barnes, "The Date of Ignatius", *Expository Times* 120 (2008), pp. 119–130, argues for the 140s. The letter of Polycarp, who knew Ignatius and died in the 150s or 160s, sets the outer limit [[cite:polycarp-phil]].

[[person:ignatius-of-antioch]] calls himself "the bishop of Syria" [[cite:rom-2]]. His letters do not say who arrested him or on what charge. They show him as a condemned man on the road from [[place:antioch]] in Syria to [[place:rome]], chained to a squad of ten soldiers whom he calls "ten leopards", and expecting to be thrown to wild beasts when he arrives [[cite:smyrn-11]] [[cite:rom-5]] [[cite:eph-1]].

The road led through Asia Minor. At [[place:smyrna]], where [[person:polycarp]] was bishop, he wrote to the churches of [[place:ephesus]], Magnesia and Tralles, whose delegates had come to meet him, and to the church of Rome [[cite:eph-21]] [[cite:magn-15]] [[cite:trall-12]] [[cite:rom-10]]. From Troas he wrote to Philadelphia, to Smyrna and to Polycarp, and says that he must sail at once for Neapolis [[cite:philad-11]] [[cite:smyrn-12]] [[cite:pol-8]].

The letters press two points. The first is unity around the bishop. The bishop presides "in the place of God", the presbyters in the place of the council of the apostles, and the deacons are entrusted with the service of Jesus Christ [[cite:magn-6]]. Nothing that concerns the church is to be done without the bishop; that is to be counted "a proper Eucharist" which is held by the bishop or by one to whom he has entrusted it; and where the bishop appears, there the people are to be, "even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church" [[cite:smyrn-8]]. The second is that Christ was truly born, truly crucified and truly raised, against those who said he only seemed to suffer [[cite:trall-9]]. Such people, Ignatius says, stay away from the Eucharist because they do not confess it to be the flesh of Jesus Christ [[cite:smyrn-7]].

To Rome he writes differently. He greets a church that "presides in the place of the region of the Romans" and "presides over love" [[cite:rom-greeting]], and begs it not to intervene for him: "I am the wheat of God, and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, that I may be found the pure bread of Christ." He does not give them orders, he adds, as [[person:peter]] and [[person:paul]] did [[cite:rom-4]].

The letters stop before he reaches Rome. Polycarp, writing to [[place:philippi]], counts Ignatius among those who are now with the Lord, sends copies of his letters and asks for any further news [[cite:polycarp-phil]]. [[person:irenaeus]], about 180, quotes the sentence about the wheat as the words of a man condemned to the beasts [[cite:irenaeus]]. [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]], two centuries after the event, gives the death in Rome as what "report says" [[cite:eusebius]].

The churches do not read these letters alike, and only one reading is documented here. The Catechism of the Catholic Church quotes the letter to Smyrna for the bishop's office and the greeting to Rome, "which presides in charity", for the communion of the other churches with the church of Rome [[cite:ccc-896]] [[cite:ccc-834]]. No Orthodox or Protestant document that discusses these passages was found for this entry, so those readings are not represented.
