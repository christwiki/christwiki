dating: |
  The Chronicle of Edessa dates the flood to the month of Second Tishrin (November) in the year 513 of the Seleucid era, which began in October AD 201 [[cite:flood]]. The chronicle was compiled in the sixth century, but this entry reproduces a report signed by the city's scribes and archivists. It shows a church building in Edessa by 201; when the first Christians came there is not known, and the event is placed at this year for that reason.

  B. H. Cowper, who translated the chronicle, took the notice as proof that the Christians of Edessa had a house of worship by that date (note to entry 106 on the linked page). Walter Bauer suggested that the words about the church were added by the sixth-century compiler, and that the correspondence of Abgar and Jesus is a legend composed in Edessa in the time of its bishop Conon, shortly before Eusebius wrote (*Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity*, 1934; English translation 1971, chapter 1). The document that Eusebius translates dates the story to "the three hundred and fortieth year", which by the Seleucid era used at Edessa is about AD 29 [[cite:abgar]]. A. C. McGiffert, in the notes to the linked translation, calls Eusebius the first writer to mention the letters and judges them a later forgery; they are not treated here as a record of the first century.

[[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]] calls [[place:edessa]] a royal city, whose king ruled beyond the Euphrates and whose records were kept in Syriac [[cite:abgar]]. He also knew a letter from the churches of its district, Osrhoene, written during the dispute about the date of Easter near the end of the second century [[cite:easter]].

In November 201 the river that ran through the city burst its banks at night. A report written by the city's scribes and deposited in its archives, and copied centuries later into the Chronicle of Edessa, tells how King [[person:abgar-viii]] watched from a tower by torchlight while the western wall gave way. The water wrecked his palace and the buildings along the river and drowned more than two thousand people. Among the losses the report lists "the temple of the church of the Christians" [[cite:flood]].

The Christian teacher [[person:bardaisan]], whose birth in 154 the city's chronicle records, belongs to these years [[cite:birth]]. Eusebius says that he wrote dialogues in Syriac against the followers of [[person:marcion]], at a time when rival teachings abounded in Mesopotamia [[cite:bardaisan]]. A dialogue from his school speaks of "the new race of us Christians", who meet on the first day of the week, and names believers in Parthia, Persia, Media and Edessa. It also says that King Abgar, on becoming a believer, forbade a rite of self-castration performed in honour of a goddess [[cite:laws]]. It does not say which king of that name is meant.

By the time Eusebius wrote, early in the fourth century, a story was told that traced the church of Edessa to the lifetime of Jesus. Eusebius reports that an earlier King Abgar, being ill, wrote to ask Jesus to come and heal him; that Jesus answered by letter, promising to send a disciple; and that after the ascension Thaddaeus, one of the seventy, came to Edessa, cured the king and preached to the citizens. Eusebius says that he translated the letters from Syriac documents in the city's archives [[cite:abgar]]. The city's own chronicle, which draws on those archives, has no such story, and the first bishop it names is Conon, who began to build a church in the Seleucid year 624, AD 312–313 [[cite:bishop]].

What the flood report and the writings of Bardaisan's circle show is a Christian community settled in Edessa and writing in Syriac by the opening of the third century [[cite:flood]] [[cite:laws]]. The story of Abgar's letter, which Eusebius passed on to Greek readers, is an account of those beginnings that was current in his day [[cite:abgar]].
