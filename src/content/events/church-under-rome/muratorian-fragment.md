dating: |
  The manuscript is dated by the library that holds it to AD 676–750 [[cite:codex]]. The list it copies is older, and how much older is disputed. The main clue in the text is its remark that Hermas wrote the *Shepherd* "very recently in our times in the city of Rome", when his brother Pius was bishop there [[cite:others]]. Eusebius places Pius between Hyginus and Anicetus, in the 140s and 150s [[cite:pius]]. The usual conclusion is that the list was drawn up at or near Rome a generation later, about 170–200, and its rejection of the "Cataphrygians" fits a time when Montanism was a live issue.

  Albert C. Sundberg, "Canon Muratori: A Fourth-Century List", *Harvard Theological Review* 66 (1973), pp. 1–41, and Geoffrey Mark Hahneman, *The Muratorian Fragment and the Development of the Canon* (1992), argue instead that it is an eastern list of the fourth century, when comparable catalogues first appear. Everett Ferguson, "Canon Muratori: Date and Provenance", *Studia Patristica* 17.2 (1982), pp. 677–683, defends the earlier date, which most scholars still hold. Eckhard J. Schnabel, "The Muratorian Fragment: The State of Research", *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 57 (2014), pp. 231–264, reviews the debate.

The text is named after Ludovico Antonio Muratori, who printed it in 1740 from a manuscript in the Biblioteca Ambrosiana in [[place:milan]]. It fills three pages of a codex of the late seventh or eighth century that otherwise contains works of Eucherius, Ambrose and others [[cite:codex]]. The text begins in the middle of a sentence [[cite:gospels]]. Because the author speaks of [[place:rome]] simply as "the city", the list is usually thought to come from there [[cite:acts]].

What survives opens with the end of a remark about one Gospel and then calls that of [[person:luke]] "the third book of the Gospel" and that of [[person:john-the-apostle|John]], "one of the disciples", the fourth, so the lost beginning dealt with two others. John, it says, wrote at the urging of his fellow disciples and bishops after a fast and a revelation to the apostle [[person:andrew]]. The Gospels differ in what they teach, but "there is no difference as regards the faith of believers", since one Spirit declares in all of them the Lord's birth, passion, resurrection and two comings [[cite:gospels]].

Next come the Acts of the Apostles, written by Luke [[cite:acts]], and the letters of [[person:paul]]. He wrote to seven churches by name, as John did in the Apocalypse, and so addressed the one church spread through the world; with the letters to Philemon, Titus and Timothy this makes thirteen. Letters to the Laodiceans and Alexandrians are rejected as forgeries in Paul's name connected with the heresy of [[person:marcion]], "for it is not suitable for gall to be mingled with honey" [[cite:paul]].

The list then names Jude, two letters of John, the Wisdom of Solomon, and the Apocalypses of John and of Peter, "though some among us will not have this latter read in the Church". The *Shepherd* of Hermas may be read, but not publicly among the prophets or the apostles, because it was written recently. The writings of Valentinus, Basilides, Marcion's followers and the "Cataphrygians" are refused [[cite:others]]. Hebrews, James and the letters of Peter are not mentioned.

The fragment shows a church that already treated four Gospels, Acts and thirteen letters of Paul as settled, while the edges of the collection were still open. It tests a book by whether it comes from the apostles' time and whether it is read in the whole church. For a list of exactly the twenty-seven books of the later New Testament, see [[event:athanasius-festal-letter]], of the year 367.
