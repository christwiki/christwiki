dating: |
  The chronology comes from Eusebius. He puts the death of Origen's father in the tenth year of the emperor Severus, AD 202–203, when Origen was not quite seventeen, and his appointment as teacher in his eighteenth year [[cite:youth]] [[cite:school]]. That gives a birth about 185 and a beginning about 203. The move to Caesarea is dated to the tenth year of Alexander Severus, about 231 [[cite:move]].

  Eusebius says that Origen died aged sixty-nine in the reign of Gallus, who ruled from 251 to 253 [[cite:death]]. Sixty-nine years from a birth about 185 lead to 254 or 255, just after Gallus, so the two statements do not quite agree and the year of death is given as about 254.

[[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]] says that [[person:clement-of-alexandria]] had charge of the instruction of converts in the church at [[place:alexandria]] and that [[person:origen]], as a boy, was among his pupils [[cite:clement]]. In the persecution under Severus Origen's father was beheaded. Origen, not yet seventeen, wanted to give himself up and was kept at home only because his mother hid his clothes [[cite:youth]]. In his eighteenth year the bishop, Demetrius, entrusted the teaching of converts to him. He sold his books of Greek literature for a small daily allowance, fasted, and slept on the floor [[cite:school]]. Eusebius also reports, as a rash act of his youth, that he took literally the saying about those who make themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven [[bible:Matt 19:12]] [[cite:eunuch]].

His life's work was the Bible. He learned Hebrew, collected the Greek translations and set them in parallel columns beside the Hebrew text in the work called the Hexapla [[cite:hexapla]]. A wealthy friend, Ambrose, paid for more than seven shorthand writers and as many copyists, and the commentaries followed [[cite:ambrose]]. In *On First Principles* he separated what the apostles had taught plainly, about God, Christ and the Spirit, from the questions they had left open, on which he offered his own reasoning; and he held that Scripture has a meaning beyond the obvious one [[cite:preface]]. A reader, he wrote, should find in it three levels, like the body, soul and spirit of a person [[cite:senses]]. The pagan philosopher Porphyry, in a passage that Eusebius quotes, says that he met Origen in his youth and complains that he applied to the Jewish Scriptures the figurative methods that Greek writers used on their own mysteries [[cite:porphyry]].

On a journey he was ordained presbyter by the bishops of Palestine, and Demetrius turned against him [[cite:ordination]]. He left Alexandria for [[place:caesarea-maritima]] [[cite:move]], where students came to him from other provinces and where, past sixty, he wrote his reply to the pagan critic Celsus [[cite:caesarea]]. In the persecution of [[person:decius]] he was chained, racked and threatened with fire [[cite:torture]]. He died not long afterwards, aged sixty-nine [[cite:death]].

Origen's work bears on what the church read as Scripture and on how it read it. Eusebius quotes his list of the twenty-two books of the Hebrew Scriptures and his statement that four Gospels alone are undisputed in the church [[cite:canon]]. His opinions were contested after his death: when Rufinus put *On First Principles* into Latin more than a century later, he explained in his preface that he had left out or altered passages on the Trinity that he judged to be corrupt [[cite:rufinus]].
