  The sources give two dates. Epiphanius says that the "Phrygians" arose about the nineteenth year of Antoninus Pius, AD 156–157 [[cite:epiph-date]]. The *Chronicle* of Eusebius, in Jerome's Latin version, enters the beginning of the "Cataphrygian" prophecy under the eleventh year of Marcus Aurelius, AD 171 [[cite:chronicle]]. The unnamed writer whom Eusebius quotes says only that Montanus began when one Gratus was proconsul of Asia, and he gives no year for Gratus [[cite:anon-origin]].

  The other writers do not settle the matter. Apollonius wrote in the fortieth year after Montanus began to prophesy [[cite:apollonius-date]], and the unnamed writer more than thirteen years after Maximilla died [[cite:anon-date]], but neither says in what year he was writing. By the time of the persecution at Lyon, which Eusebius dates to 177, the dispute had reached Gaul and Rome [[cite:lyon]]. Historians are divided between the two dates. Christine Trevett, *Montanism: Gender, Authority and the New Prophecy* (1996), reviews the arguments for each; William Tabbernee, *Fake Prophecy and Polluted Sacraments: Ecclesiastical and Imperial Reactions to Montanism* (2007), places the beginning about 165. Almost everything known about the "New Prophecy" comes from its opponents. [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]], writing more than a century later, quotes two of them at length, an unnamed writer of Asia Minor and a certain Apollonius [[cite:anon-origin]] [[cite:apollonius]]. Epiphanius, two centuries after the events, preserves another attack together with some sayings of the prophets [[cite:epiph-end]]. No English translation of his book is in the public domain, and what he says is paraphrased here from the Greek text to which the citations link. A friendly witness is [[person:tertullian]], who took the side of the prophecy [[cite:praxeas]].

The unnamed writer passes on a report that it began at Ardabau, a village on the border of Mysia and Phrygia. By this account a recent convert, [[person:montanus]], fell into a trance and began to utter strange things, "contrary to the constant custom of the Church". Some hearers rebuked him as possessed; others took it for the Holy Spirit. Two women were seized in the same way [[cite:anon-origin]]. Eusebius names them as Priscilla and [[person:maximilla]] [[cite:eus-14]]. Their followers spoke of "the new prophecy" [[cite:serapion]]. In a saying that Epiphanius quotes, Montanus speaks in the first person as the one who inspires: a man is like a lyre and the speaker comes upon him like a plectrum; the man sleeps and the speaker keeps watch; it is the Lord who puts men's hearts into ecstasy [[cite:epiph-lyre]].

Apollonius charges that Montanus taught the dissolving of marriages, made rules for fasts, appointed collectors of money, and gave the name Jerusalem to [[place:pepuza]] and Tymion, two small towns of Phrygia, where he wanted people to gather [[cite:apollonius]]. Epiphanius reports the story that a prophetess, Quintilla or Priscilla (he says he does not know which), slept at Pepuza and said that Christ came to her there in the form of a woman and revealed to her that the place was holy and that Jerusalem would come down to it from heaven [[cite:epiph-pepuza]]. He also quotes a saying of Maximilla that after her there would be no more prophets, only the end [[cite:epiph-end]]. He grants that the group accepted the whole of the Old and New Testaments and the resurrection of the dead [[cite:epiph-scripture]].

The same writer says that the Christians of Asia met in many places, examined the sayings, judged them profane and shut their adherents out of communion [[cite:anon-synods]]. Bishops who came to test the spirit in Maximilla were kept from doing so, and she complained: "I am driven away from the sheep like a wolf. I am not a wolf. I am word and spirit and power" [[cite:anon-maximilla]]. The writer repeats a rumour that Montanus and Maximilla hanged themselves, and adds that he does not know whether it is true [[cite:anon-deaths]].

The question went beyond Asia. During the persecution at [[place:lyon]] (see [[event:martyrs-of-lyon]]) the Christians of Gaul wrote about it to Asia, Phrygia and Rome [[cite:lyon]]. Tertullian says that a bishop of Rome had recognised the prophecies and then, persuaded by one Praxeas, withdrew his letter of peace, and that he himself broke with his opponents over "the Paraclete" [[cite:praxeas]].

The dispute made the churches ask whether new prophecy could stand beside what the apostles had handed down. The unnamed writer says that he long hesitated to write at all, for fear of seeming to add to "the Gospel of the New Testament" [[cite:anon-intro]].
