dating: |
  The year comes from Eusebius, who introduces the letter by saying that persecution broke out again "in the seventeenth year of the Emperor Antoninus Verus", that is of Marcus Aurelius, and that Eleutherus was then bishop of Rome [[cite:preface]]. Marcus became emperor in March 161, so his seventeenth year began in March 177. The letter as Eusebius quotes it names neither year nor emperor; it speaks only of "Caesar" [[cite:caesar]].

  Eusebius is not consistent. His *Chronicle*, in Jerome's version, enters the deaths in Gaul ten years earlier, beside that of Polycarp [[cite:chronicle]]. The *Church History*, where he had the letter before him, is generally followed, and 177 is the accepted date.

[[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]], writing in the fourth century, preserves long extracts from a letter sent by "the servants of Christ residing at Vienne and Lyons, in Gaul" to their fellow Christians in Asia and Phrygia [[cite:address]].

As the letter tells it, trouble began in the streets. Christians at [[place:lyon]] were barred from houses, baths and markets and set upon by the crowd, then questioned in the forum by the city authorities and held for the governor. When he arrived, a young man of rank, Vettius Epagathus, asked to speak for the accused; the governor asked only whether he too was a Christian, and he was put with the rest. About ten gave way. Pagan slaves of Christian households, afraid of torture, accused their masters of cannibal feasts and incest, and the city turned savage [[cite:outbreak]].

The letter dwells on those who held out. Sanctus, a deacon from Vienne, answered every question with "I am a Christian." [[person:blandina]], a slave whose mistress was also on trial, wore out her torturers and kept saying, "I am a Christian, and there is nothing vile done by us." Many died in the prison [[cite:witnesses]]. [[person:pothinus]], the bishop of Lyon, more than ninety years old, was beaten before the tribunal and died in prison two days later [[cite:pothinus]].

The governor wrote to the emperor, and the reply was that those who denied should be released and the rest put to death. At a festival that drew crowds from many nations, the Roman citizens were beheaded and the others sent to the beasts; Attalus of Pergamum, a citizen, was sent to the beasts as well to please the crowd [[cite:caesar]]. Blandina had already been hung on a stake in the amphitheatre and left untouched by the animals [[cite:arena]]. On the last day she saw Ponticus, a boy of about fifteen, die, and was then scourged, burned, tied in a net and thrown to a bull [[cite:blandina]]. The bodies were left exposed for six days, burned, and swept into the Rhône, to mock the hope of resurrection [[cite:bodies]].

While they lived, the prisoners refused to be called martyrs, keeping that name for Christ and for those already dead [[cite:confessors]]. Through them many who had denied the faith came back and confessed it [[cite:restored]]. Eusebius adds that from prison they also wrote to Asia and Phrygia and to Eleutherus of Rome about the prophecy of Montanus (see [[event:montanism]]) [[cite:montanism]]. They sent their letter to Rome by the presbyter [[person:irenaeus]], who succeeded Pothinus as bishop [[cite:irenaeus]].
