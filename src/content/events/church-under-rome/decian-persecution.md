  Two documents fix the year. The fourth-century list of Roman bishops places Fabian's death in January of the consulship of Decius, for the second time, and Gratus, which is AD 250 [[cite:catalogue]]. The certificate of sacrifice cited here is dated 23 Pauni in the first year of Decius, which falls in June 250 [[cite:libellus]]. The order to sacrifice was therefore in force from the first weeks of 250 and was still being applied in Egypt in the summer. Decius reigned less than two years and was succeeded by Gallus [[cite:reign]]; the same list of bishops names Decius as consul for 251 and Gallus as consul for 252 [[cite:catalogue]].

[[person:decius]] became emperor in succession to Philip [[cite:motive]]. Soon afterwards an order went out that people should sacrifice to the gods. None of the writers cited here quotes the order, and what it demanded is seen in certificates on papyrus that survive from Egypt. In one of them, dated June 250, a villager and his two children declare before the local commission for sacrifices that they have always sacrificed to the gods and have now done so in the commissioners' presence, poured a libation and tasted the offerings; two officials sign as witnesses [[cite:libellus]]. The certificate does not mention Christians. Christian writers took the measure as an attack on the church, and [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]] puts it down to Decius's hatred of his predecessor [[cite:motive]].

At [[place:rome]] the bishop, [[person:fabian]], died in January 250, and two presbyters and a deacon were imprisoned [[cite:catalogue]] [[cite:feast]]. The list dates his successor from the consuls of the following year [[cite:catalogue]]; Cyprian wrote that the emperor, whom he calls a tyrant, would rather have heard of a rival emperor than of a new bishop at Rome [[cite:rival]]. In the East, Eusebius reports, the bishops of Jerusalem and Antioch died in prison, and [[person:origen]] was tortured [[cite:east]].

Dionysius, bishop of [[place:alexandria]], in a letter that Eusebius quotes, described what happened when the decree arrived there. Many of the more prominent Christians came forward at once; others went because their public offices required it, or were pressed by their friends; called by name, they went up to the sacrifices, some of them pale and shaking. Others fled or were arrested, and of those some gave way after days in prison or under torture. Some held out and were burned or beheaded [[cite:dionysius]].

[[person:cyprian]], bishop of [[place:carthage]], went into hiding when the crowd called for him, and governed his church by letter [[cite:withdrawal]]. On his return he said that most of his people had denied their faith at the first threat, that some had hurried to the forum before they were summoned, and that parents had carried their infants with them to the altar [[cite:lapsed]]. Others had obtained a certificate without sacrificing. He counted that a lesser fault but still a denial [[cite:certificates]].

Cyprian opened his address to his church with the words that peace had been restored [[cite:peace]]. Decius was killed in battle after a reign of less than two years [[cite:death]] [[cite:reign]]. The order had been applied at Rome and in Africa, Egypt, Syria and Palestine, and it left behind baptised Christians who had sacrificed or obtained certificates and now asked to return. A council of African bishops ruled that those with certificates might be readmitted after their cases were examined, and that those who had sacrificed should do penance and be reconciled when in danger of death [[cite:council]]. The dispute that followed is told under [[event:cyprian-on-unity]].
