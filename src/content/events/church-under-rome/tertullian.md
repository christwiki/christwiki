dating: |
  Two of Tertullian's works can be dated from what they say. The *Apology* speaks of the rebels Niger and Albinus and of their supporters as still being rounded up [[cite:apol-35]], which puts it just after Septimius Severus won the civil war in 197. The first book of *Against Marcion* names its own year, the fifteenth of Severus, AD 207–208 [[cite:marcion-date]]. The order and dates of the other treatises are worked out from cross-references and from how far each reflects his adherence to the New Prophecy.

  Jerome, writing about 393, says that Tertullian flourished under Severus and Caracalla, who ruled between 193 and 217, and that he lived to a great age [[cite:jerome]]. The latest of his surviving works are usually put at about 220, and no source gives the year of his death. The range shown is that of his writing.

Most of what is told of [[person:tertullian]]'s life comes from a short notice by [[person:jerome]], written almost two centuries later. It calls him a presbyter from [[place:carthage]], the son of a centurion, who was active under the emperors Severus and Caracalla, went over to the teaching of [[person:montanus]] in middle life and is said to have lived to old age. Jerome ranks him as chief of the Latin Christian writers after Victor and Apollonius [[cite:jerome]].

The *Apology* is addressed to the governors who tried Christians. It protests that they are condemned for a name, without the inquiry given to any other accused [[cite:apol-1]]. "We are but of yesterday, and we have filled every place among you" [[cite:apol-37]]. It describes their meetings for prayer, including prayer for the emperors, for the reading of Scripture and for discipline, and a monthly voluntary collection for the poor, orphans, the aged and prisoners, so that outsiders remark "how they love one another" [[cite:apol-39]]. Whatever disaster strikes, the cry is "Away with the Christians to the lion!" [[cite:apol-40]]. Persecution defeats itself: "the blood of Christians is seed" [[cite:apol-50]]. In a later appeal to the governor Scapula he argues that it is "a fundamental human right" for each person to worship as he is convinced, and that religion cannot be compelled [[cite:scapula]].

Against rival teachers he argued from the churches' descent from the apostles. Truth is what the churches received from the apostles, the apostles from Christ and Christ from God, and those outside that line have no claim on the Scriptures [[cite:presc-21]]. He sums up the "rule of faith" in a few lines [[cite:presc-13]] and asks, "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" [[cite:presc-7]]

His treatise *Against Praxeas* answers a teacher who, as Tertullian reports him, held that the Father himself was born and suffered [[cite:prax-1]]. There is one only God, Tertullian writes, but under a "dispensation" (in Greek *oikonomia*) "which distributes the Unity into a Trinity", Father, Son and Holy Spirit: three "not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect; yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power" [[cite:prax-2]]. One is other than another "on the ground of Personality, not of Substance" [[cite:prax-12]]. His Latin words in these passages are *trinitas*, *substantia* and *persona* [[cite:prax-latin]].

By then he had parted from many of his fellow Christians. He says that he and his friends "withdrew" over their recognition of the Paraclete, the Spirit speaking in the New Prophecy (see [[event:montanism]]) [[cite:prax-1]]. By Jerome's account he "lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus" and wrote books against the church [[cite:jerome]].
