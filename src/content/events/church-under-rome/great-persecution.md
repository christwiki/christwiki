[[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]] describes the years before the persecution, under [[person:diocletian]], as a time of favour: Christians governed provinces and served in the imperial household, and congregations had outgrown their old buildings and were putting up large churches in every city [[cite:peace]]. Lactantius, who taught rhetoric at the emperor's residence, [[place:nicomedia]] [[cite:lactantius-life]], says the change began when Christian attendants made the sign of the cross at a sacrifice and the soothsayers blamed them for its failure. In his account Diocletian then ordered the palace staff and the army to sacrifice, his junior colleague [[person:galerius]] urged a general persecution through the winter, and an oracle of Apollo decided the matter [[cite:origins]].

On 23 February 303, Lactantius relates, officials broke into the church at Nicomedia, burned the Scriptures they found and had the building levelled [[cite:church]]. The edict posted next day, he says, stripped Christians of rank and of the protection of the courts [[cite:edict]]. Eusebius gives its other terms: churches were to be razed and the Scriptures burned [[cite:eusebius-edict]]. Lactantius tells of two fires in the palace within fifteen days and accuses Galerius of having them set; Eusebius mentions a fire and says he does not know how it began. Both say that the Christians were blamed and that members of the household and clergy of the city were put to death [[cite:fire]] [[cite:eusebius-fire]].

More edicts followed. The clergy everywhere were to be imprisoned and then forced by torture to sacrifice [[cite:eusebius-edict]] [[cite:eusebius-fire]]. In the second year, Eusebius reports from Palestine, a general order required all the people in every city to sacrifice and pour libations [[cite:sacrifice]].

An official record of the first edict at work is preserved among the papers appended to the work of Optatus. On 19 May 303 the curator of the city of Cirta in Numidia went to "the house in which the Christians were accustomed to assemble" and demanded the Scriptures. The bishop said that the readers had them. The clerk listed what was handed over, chalices of gold and silver, lamps, and stores of clothing, and the search went on from house to house as books were handed over [[cite:cirta]]. Clergy who surrendered the Scriptures in this way were afterwards called traitors, and the charge lay behind the [[event:donatist-schism]].

Enforcement was uneven. In Gaul the junior emperor Constantius, according to Lactantius, let church buildings be pulled down but harmed no one; Eusebius says he did not even do that [[cite:constantius]] [[cite:eusebius-constantius]]. Eusebius says that [[person:maxentius]], when he gained power at Rome, ordered the persecution to stop [[cite:maxentius]]. Eusebius reckons that the western provinces suffered for less than two years and Palestine for eight [[cite:west]]. Galerius, dying, issued an edict of toleration in 311 [[cite:end]]; see [[event:edict-of-galerius]].

The narrative comes from two Christian writers, Lactantius and Eusebius, who lived through these years and wrote as advocates. The record from Cirta was made by the officials who carried the edict out.
