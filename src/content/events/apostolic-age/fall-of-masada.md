dating: |
  Josephus gives the day, the fifteenth of the month Xanthicus, and no year [[cite:end]]. The last date he gives before it is the fourth year of Vespasian, for another event told earlier in the same book [[cite:fourth-year]]. From that the year 73 was inferred, and the World Heritage listing gives AD 73 for the fall [[cite:works-today]]. Werner Eck, "Die Eroberung von Masada und eine neue Inschrift des L. Flavius Silva Nonius Bassus", *Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft* 60 (1969), pp. 282–289, argued from the order of the offices in Silva's inscription [[cite:inscription]] that he cannot have become governor of Judaea before the spring of 73, and so proposed the spring of 74 for the fall of Masada. Eck's argument and the older inference are given here as Hannah M. Cotton reports them in "The Date of the Fall of Masada: The Evidence of the Masada Papyri", *Zeitschrift für Papyrologie und Epigraphik* 78 (1989), pp. 157–162. Cotton sets against the inscription papyri found at Masada that give circumstantial support to 73, and concludes that the evidence cannot decide the issue. Jonathan Roth, "The Length of the Siege of Masada", *Scripta Classica Israelica* 14 (1995), pp. 87–110, calls the choice between 73 and 74 a matter of controversy and lists the literature. The timeline places the event at 73 and shows both years.

  Roth notes that only one account of the siege survives, that of Josephus. How far his account of the last night can be trusted is also debated. Shaye J. D. Cohen, "Masada: Literary Tradition, Archaeological Remains, and the Credibility of Josephus", *Journal of Jewish Studies* 33 (1982), pp. 385–405, sets it beside the finds of Yigael Yadin's excavation of 1963–1965. He notes that Yadin found three skeletons in the northern palace and twenty-five in a cave on the southern cliff, and traces of many separate fires, where Josephus has 960 dead in the palace and a single heap set alight. Cohen concludes that some of the defenders did kill themselves, that Josephus enlarged and embellished this, and that Eleazar's speeches are Josephus's own composition.

  Jodi Magness, *Masada: From Jewish Revolt to Modern Myth* (2019), holds that archaeology is not equipped to settle whether the mass suicide took place. She reports that Yadin took a group of twelve potsherds inscribed with names, one of them "ben Yair", for the lots of Josephus's story, and that Joseph Naveh, who published them, could not identify them conclusively as such.

At the outbreak of the revolt in AD 66, [[person:josephus]] says, rebels took the fortress of [[place:masada]] by treachery and killed its Roman garrison [[cite:seized]]. When the rebel leader Menahem was killed in Jerusalem, his kinsman [[person:eleazar-ben-yair|Eleazar son of Jair]] escaped there [[cite:eleazar]]. Josephus calls the holders of Masada Sicarii and says that they raided the country round about. At Engedi, one Passover, they killed more than seven hundred women and children [[cite:engedi]].

Masada was still held [[event:destruction-of-the-second-temple|after Jerusalem fell]]. When the governor Bassus died, [[person:flavius-silva]] succeeded him and marched against it, the one stronghold still in revolt [[cite:silva]]. He built a wall round the whole rock so that no one could escape, and pitched his camp [[cite:works]]. On a spur on the western side, called the White Promontory, his men raised an embankment with a platform of stone on top, and brought up an iron-plated tower and a ram. When the ram broke the wall, the defenders built a second one of timber and earth, and Silva had it set on fire [[cite:ramp]].

Josephus then gives Eleazar two long speeches urging his men to die free rather than be enslaved [[cite:speeches]]. The men, he says, killed their wives and children. Ten chosen by lot killed the rest, and one of the ten killed the other nine, set fire to the palace and ran himself through. By his count nine hundred and sixty died; two women and five children, who had hidden underground, survived. It was the fifteenth of the month Xanthicus [[cite:end]]. The Romans, entering the next morning, found silence and fire, and learned from the women what had been done [[cite:entry]]. Silva left a garrison and returned to Caesarea [[cite:garrison]].

The narrative rests on Josephus alone. By his own account he had left for Rome with [[person:titus]] after the war in Judaea [[cite:rome]], and he names the two women as the source of what the Romans learned [[cite:entry]]. Pliny the Elder mentions Masada as a fortress on a rock near the Dead Sea [[cite:pliny]]. Inscriptions that Silva set up at Urbs Salvia in Italy list his offices, and one preserves, but for its first two letters, the phrase "of the province of Judaea" [[cite:inscription]]. Roman camps, fortifications and an assault ramp still surround the rock [[cite:works-today]]. What the excavations of the 1960s found, and how scholars weigh Josephus's story against it, is set out in the note on dating.

Josephus presents the fall of Masada as the end of the war in Judaea: no enemy, he says, was left in the country [[cite:garrison]].
