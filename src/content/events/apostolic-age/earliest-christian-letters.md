  The letters carry no dates. They are dated by fitting what they say about Paul's movements into the framework of Acts, which the Gallio inscription anchors at Corinth in about AD 51–52 (see [[event:second-missionary-journey]]). 1 Thessalonians was written when Timothy had just come back from Thessalonica [[bible:1 Thess 3:6]], which matches the stay at Corinth of about AD 50–51 [[bible:Acts 18:5]]. 1 Corinthians was written at Ephesus, 2 Corinthians after Paul had gone on to Macedonia, and Romans when he was about to carry the collection to Jerusalem [[bible:1 Cor 16:8]] [[bible:2 Cor 7:5]] [[bible:Rom 15:25-26]]; these belong to the years about 53–57 (see [[event:third-missionary-journey]]). Philippians and Philemon were written in custody, in a place they do not name [[bible:Phil 1:13]] [[bible:Phlm 1]]. The date of Galatians depends on which churches it addresses and which Jerusalem visit it describes; see [[event:council-of-jerusalem]]. Rainer Riesner, *Paul's Early Period: Chronology, Mission Strategy, Theology* (1998), dates it to 48, before 1 Thessalonians; Raymond E. Brown, in the work named below, takes 1 Thessalonians to be the first of the letters.

  Authorship. Thirteen letters bear Paul's name. Seven are generally treated as undisputed: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon. The other six are disputed: 2 Thessalonians, Colossians, Ephesians, 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus. Some scholars hold that Paul wrote them; others that followers wrote them in his name after his death. Raymond E. Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (1997), treats the seven as undisputed and discusses the six in ascending order of doubt, ending with the letters to Timothy and Titus. D. A. Carson and Douglas J. Moo, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (2nd ed., 2005), defend Paul's authorship of the letters to Timothy and Titus and contest Brown's estimate of how many scholars reject Ephesians. This wiki does not take a side. Hebrews names no author.

[[person:paul]] wrote to his churches when he could not be with them [[bible:2 Cor 13:10]]. The first letter to the Thessalonians opens in the name of "Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy". It was written after [[person:timothy]], whom Paul had sent back from [[place:athens|Athens]], returned with good news of the new church, and it ends with the charge that it be read to all the believers [[bible:1 Thess 1:1]] [[bible:1 Thess 3:1-6]] [[bible:1 Thess 5:27]]. On the usual dating it was written about AD 50–51 at [[place:corinth]], earlier than the Gospels as most scholars date them; see [[event:writing-of-the-gospels]].

The others likewise answer to particular needs. Galatians is a protest to churches that were turning to "a different good news" [[bible:Gal 1:6-9]]. 1 Corinthians, written at [[place:ephesus]], replies to reports of division and to questions the Corinthians had put in writing [[bible:1 Cor 1:11]] [[bible:1 Cor 7:1]] [[bible:1 Cor 16:8]]. Romans introduces Paul and his message to a church he had not yet visited, as he was about to take a collection to [[place:jerusalem|Jerusalem]] and hoped to go on to Spain [[bible:Rom 1:8-15]] [[bible:Rom 15:23-28]].

The letters show how they were made. Several name co-senders [[bible:1 Cor 1:1]] [[bible:Phil 1:1]]. Paul dictated at least some of them: the scribe of Romans adds his own greeting, "I, Tertius, who write the letter", and Paul closes other letters with a line in his own hand [[bible:Rom 16:22]] [[bible:1 Cor 16:21]] [[bible:Gal 6:11]]. The letter to the Colossians asks that it be read at Laodicea as well and exchanged for a letter from there [[bible:Col 4:16]].

They hold early written statements of what the first Christians believed. Paul reminds the Corinthians of what he had himself "received" and passed on: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to [[person:peter|Cephas]], to the Twelve and to many others [[bible:1 Cor 15:3-8]]. Romans sets out its theme as the good news that is "the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes" [[bible:Rom 1:16-17]].

Thirteen letters in the New Testament bear Paul's name. 2 Thessalonians, itself one of the disputed letters, warns against a letter "as if from us" and points to the greeting in Paul's own hand as the mark of a real one [[bible:2 Thess 2:2]] [[bible:2 Thess 3:17]]. Seven of the thirteen are generally treated as undisputed, and scholars are divided over the other six; the dating note lists them and names works that discuss them.

The letters came to be known beyond the churches they were written to, and to be gathered. About AD 96 the church of [[place:rome|Rome]] could tell the Corinthians, "Take up the epistle of the blessed Apostle Paul" [[cite:clement]]. 2 Peter speaks of "all of his letters" and sets them beside "the other Scriptures" [[bible:2 Pet 3:15-16]]. The papyrus book known as Papyrus 46 is divided between two libraries. The University of Michigan calls it the oldest known copy of Paul's writings, and dates it about AD 200 on one of its pages and to the third century on another [[cite:p46-michigan]] [[cite:p46-intro]]. The Chester Beatty in Dublin calls it the earliest surviving collection of his letters and labels its leaf third century [[cite:p46-dublin]]. Michigan's description gives the contents in this order: Romans, Hebrews, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Ephesians, Galatians, Philippians, Colossians and 1 Thessalonians, with the last leaves lost [[cite:p46-contents]].
