dating: |
  No Gospel carries a date. The limits are set from outside: the events they narrate, about AD 30, and their earliest use by other writers. Papias, early in the second century, already knows writings of Mark and Matthew [[cite:papias]], and a papyrus leaf of John survives from the second century [[cite:p52]]. Within those limits the arguments turn on how the Gospels depend on one another and on whether the sayings about the temple [[bible:Mark 13:1-2]] [[bible:Luke 21:20-24]] were written down before or after its destruction in AD 70.

  Most scholars put Mark first, close to the year 70; Adela Yarbro Collins, *Mark: A Commentary* (2007), dates it during the Jewish war, before the temple fell. Raymond E. Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (1997), puts Matthew at 80–90, give or take a decade, and reports 70–100 as the majority view; in *An Introduction to the Gospel of John* (2003) he sets the final form of John between 90 and 110. A minority dates all four before 70: John A. T. Robinson, *Redating the New Testament* (1976), and Jonathan Bernier, *Rethinking the Dates of the New Testament* (2022), who puts Mark in the 40s. Irenaeus himself places Matthew in the years when Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome, and Mark after their departure [[cite:irenaeus]].

  The range shown, about 65–100, is the majority view and no more than that. Whether the traditional names are those of the actual writers is debated in the same way, and this entry does not decide it. Rome and Ephesus are shown as places only because Clement of Alexandria and Irenaeus name them [[cite:clement]] [[cite:irenaeus]]; where each Gospel was written is not known.

None of the four Gospels names its author in its text. Two of them say something about how they came to be written. Luke's preface says that many had already undertaken to draw up an account, that the matter had been handed down by those who were eyewitnesses from the beginning, and that the writer, having traced everything carefully, is setting it out in order for a certain Theophilus [[bible:Luke 1:1-4]]. John says that it was written "that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God", and a closing note ascribes it to the disciple whom Jesus loved: "We know that his witness is true" [[bible:John 20:30-31]] [[bible:John 21:20, 24]].

The oldest surviving statements about the writers come from Papias of Hierapolis, early in the second century, in words preserved by [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]]. On the authority of an elder he says that Mark had been Peter's interpreter and wrote down accurately, though not in order, what he remembered of Peter's teaching, having never heard the Lord himself. Of Matthew he says that he put together "the oracles" in the Hebrew language and that each interpreted them as he could [[cite:papias]].

[[person:irenaeus]], about AD 180, gives all four. [[person:matthew]] issued a Gospel among the Hebrews in their own language while [[person:peter]] and [[person:paul]] were preaching in [[place:rome]]. After their departure [[person:john-mark|Mark]], Peter's disciple and interpreter, handed down Peter's preaching in writing. [[person:luke]], Paul's companion, recorded the gospel that Paul preached. Afterwards [[person:john-the-apostle|John]], the disciple of the Lord who had leaned on his breast, published a Gospel while living at [[place:ephesus]] [[cite:irenaeus]]. [[person:clement-of-alexandria]], about AD 200, reports a different sequence as the tradition of the elders: the Gospels with genealogies came first, Mark wrote at Rome while Peter was still alive, and John wrote last, a "spiritual Gospel" [[cite:clement]]. The ancient witnesses thus disagree about the order and, if the "departure" of which Irenaeus speaks is the death of the apostles, about whether Mark wrote before or after Peter died.

The oldest manuscript evidence is widely held to be a papyrus fragment of John 18 in the John Rylands Library, dated by its handwriting to the second century; the library adds that recent research points to a date nearer AD 200 [[cite:p52]].

For Irenaeus the four were a fixed set: he argues that there can be neither more nor fewer than four Gospels [[cite:four]]. Their dates, their order and their relation to one another have been studied ever since, and none of these questions is closed.
