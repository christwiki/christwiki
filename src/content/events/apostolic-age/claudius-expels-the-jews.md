dating: |
  Suetonius gives no date; his Life of Claudius is arranged by subject [[cite:suetonius]]. Two years are proposed.

  AD 49 rests on two things. Orosius, a Christian historian of the early fifth century, puts the expulsion in the ninth year of Claudius, which ran from January 49 to January 50 [[cite:orosius]]. He names Josephus as his authority. Dixon Slingerland, "Suetonius *Claudius* 25.4, Acts 18, and Paulus Orosius' *Historiarum adversum paganos libri VII*: Dating the Claudian Expulsion(s) of Roman Jews", *Jewish Quarterly Review* 83 (1992), pp. 127–144, finds no source for such a statement in Josephus and judges the date to be Orosius's own construction. And Acts says that Aquila and Priscilla had "recently" come from Italy when Paul reached Corinth [[bible:Acts 18:2]], an arrival that the Gallio inscription puts at about AD 50; see [[event:second-missionary-journey]]. Rainer Riesner, *Paul's Early Period: Chronology, Mission Strategy, Theology* (1998), dates the edict to 49.

  AD 41 rests on Cassius Dio, who reports under the first year of Claudius that the emperor did not expel the Jews, because they were too many, but forbade them to hold meetings [[cite:dio]]; his next year begins three chapters later [[cite:dio-year]]. Gerd Lüdemann, *Paul, Apostle to the Gentiles: Studies in Chronology* (1984), dates the order that Acts mentions to 41 on this ground. Slingerland, *Claudian Policymaking and the Early Imperial Repression of Judaism at Rome* (1997), argues that Dio and Suetonius describe two different measures. Tacitus's account of the year 49 survives and does not mention an expulsion [[cite:tacitus]]. The timeline places the event at about 49 and marks the date as an estimate.

  On Chrestus, J. C. Rolfe's note on the passage in the Loeb edition of Suetonius (1914) takes the name as another form of Christus and thinks it probable, though uncertain, that Suetonius has made an error of chronology and is not speaking of some Jew of that name. Slingerland, in the book named above, argues that nothing in the sentence points to Jesus.

In his Life of [[person:claudius]], written about AD 120, the biographer Suetonius lists among the emperor's dealings with foreign peoples one sentence about the Jews of [[place:rome]]: since they "constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus", he expelled them from the city [[cite:suetonius]]. He says nothing more about the disturbances or about Chrestus.

Acts mentions such an order. When [[person:paul]] came to [[place:corinth|Corinth]] he found there a Jew named [[person:aquila]], born in Pontus, who had recently come from Italy with his wife [[person:priscilla]], "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome". Paul stayed and worked with them, since they shared his trade of tentmaking [[bible:Acts 18:1-3]]. Acts does not say what caused the order or whether the couple were already believers in Jesus.

Two later writers add to the picture, and complicate it. Cassius Dio, in the early third century, says that Claudius did not drive the Jews out, because their numbers would have made that hard to do without a riot, but ordered them not to hold meetings while keeping their ancestral way of life [[cite:dio]]. The Christian historian Orosius, early in the fifth century, repeats Suetonius's sentence with the name written "Christus", dates the expulsion to the ninth year of Claudius, and adds that it cannot be determined whether the emperor acted against Jews who were rioting against Christ or meant Christians to be expelled as well [[cite:orosius]].

Who Chrestus was is not settled. On one reading the name is a form of "Christus", and Suetonius has passed on a confused report of disputes among the Jews of Rome about Jesus. On the other he means a man named Chrestus who was in Rome at the time. The dating note names a work for each reading. If the first is right, there were believers in Jesus in Rome within about twenty years of the crucifixion. Neither Suetonius nor Acts says so.

Paul's letter to the Romans, written some years later, greets Prisca and Aquila and the church that met in their house. The letter is addressed to a community that he had not founded and had not yet visited [[bible:Rom 16:3-5]] [[bible:Rom 1:8-13]] [[bible:Rom 15:20-24]].
