dating: |
  Acts supplies the intervals: Paul hoped to reach Jerusalem by Pentecost [[bible:Acts 20:16]], and he had been held for two years when Felix was replaced by Festus [[bible:Acts 24:27]]. Neither Acts nor Josephus gives the year of that change of governors, and everything depends on it.

  Josephus puts it under Nero and says that Felix escaped punishment because his brother Pallas then stood high in Nero's favour [[cite:festus]]. Tacitus records that Nero removed Pallas from his post in AD 55 [[cite:pallas]], and on that ground a minority of scholars have put the arrival of Festus as early as 55 or 56. Most place it between 58 and 60. Paul addresses Felix as one who has judged the nation "for many years" [[bible:Acts 24:10]], and the rising of the Egyptian, which Josephus tells among the events of Nero's reign, already lay in the past when Paul was arrested [[bible:Acts 21:38]] [[cite:egyptian-date]]. Rainer Riesner, *Paul's Early Period: Chronology, Mission Strategy, Theology* (1998), dates the arrest to 57 and the sending of Paul to Rome to 59, and the timeline follows that reckoning.

When [[person:paul]] reached [[place:jerusalem]], [[person:james-the-just|James]] and the elders told him that thousands of Jewish believers, all zealous for the law, had heard that he taught Jews to abandon Moses. On their advice he joined four men who were under a vow and paid their expenses in the temple [[bible:Acts 21:17-26]]. There Jews from Asia seized him, shouting that he had brought Greeks into the holy place. The crowd dragged him out and was beating him when the Roman tribune, Claudius Lysias, came down with soldiers and put him in chains [[bible:Acts 21:27-36]] [[bible:Acts 23:26]]. The tribune at first took him for the Egyptian who had lately led four thousand men into the wilderness [[bible:Acts 21:38]]; [[person:josephus]] tells of an Egyptian prophet, with thirty thousand followers by his count, whose following Felix scattered at the Mount of Olives [[cite:egyptian]].

Paul addressed the crowd from the barracks steps, avoided a flogging by declaring that he was a Roman citizen, and was brought before the council, where his claim to stand for the resurrection of the dead divided Pharisees from Sadducees [[bible:Acts 21:40-22:29]] [[bible:Acts 22:30-23:10]]. Learning that more than forty men had sworn to kill him, the tribune sent him by night under guard to the governor at [[place:caesarea-maritima]] [[bible:Acts 23:12-35]].

The governor was [[person:felix]], a brother of Pallas, who had married Drusilla, a sister of [[person:herod-agrippa-ii|King Agrippa]] [[cite:felix]]. Tacitus calls him a freedman and says that he exercised a king's power in the spirit of a slave [[cite:tacitus-felix]]. The high priest Ananias and an advocate charged Paul with stirring up riots as a leader of "the sect of the Nazarenes" and with trying to profane the temple; Paul replied that he had come to bring alms to his nation [[bible:Acts 24:1-21]]. Felix put off a decision, hoped for a bribe, and left Paul in custody for two years [[bible:Acts 24:22-27]].

His successor, Porcius [[person:festus]], proposed a trial in Jerusalem. Paul refused and appealed to Caesar [[bible:Acts 25:1-12]]. Before sending him, Festus set the case before Agrippa and Bernice, who was the king's sister [[cite:bernice]]. Paul told once more how he had been turned from persecutor to preacher, and Agrippa said that the man could have been released if he had not appealed [[bible:Acts 25:13-26:32]].

Acts records the judgment of the tribune, of Festus and of Agrippa that Paul had done nothing deserving death [[bible:Acts 23:29]] [[bible:Acts 25:25]] [[bible:Acts 26:31]]. The appeal took him to [[place:rome]] as a prisoner [[bible:Acts 27:1; 28:16]]. This account rests on Acts alone among the sources cited here; the passages of Josephus on the governors and the king do not mention Paul.
