dating: |
  The book gives no date. Irenaeus, about AD 180, says that the vision was seen "towards the end of Domitian's reign" [[cite:irenaeus-date]]. Domitian was killed in September 96 [[cite:domitian-death]], so this points to about 95, and most scholars accept it; so Adela Yarbro Collins, *Crisis and Catharsis: The Power of the Apocalypse* (1984), who relies on Irenaeus and does not assume a general persecution under Domitian. Leonard L. Thompson, *The Book of Revelation: Apocalypse and Empire* (1990), likewise doubts that such a persecution took place.

  A minority prefers a date about 68–70, soon after Nero's death. The arguments are drawn from the text: the temple and the holy city are spoken of as still to be trampled [[bible:Rev 11:1-2]], and of seven kings "five have fallen, the one is" [[bible:Rev 17:10]], which is read as a count of emperors ending with Nero's successor. John A. T. Robinson, *Redating the New Testament* (1976), argues this case. The timeline follows Irenaeus; the earlier date is recorded here.

The book opens as a revelation of Jesus Christ sent "to his servant, John" [[bible:Rev 1:1]]. John calls himself the brother of his readers and their partner in suffering. He was, he says, on the island called [[place:patmos]] "because of God's Word and the testimony of Jesus Christ", and was "in the Spirit on the Lord's day" when a voice told him to write what he saw and send it to seven churches of Asia, beginning with [[place:ephesus]]. He turned and saw one "like a son of man" standing among seven lampstands [[bible:Rev 1:4, 9-13]].

Seven short messages follow, each praising or rebuking one church. Ephesus has left its first love; [[place:smyrna]] is about to suffer; at Pergamum a witness named Antipas has been killed; Laodicea is lukewarm [[bible:Rev 2:4, 10, 13]] [[bible:Rev 3:16]].

The visions that fill the rest of the book show the souls of those killed for their testimony, a beast that makes war on the saints and demands worship, and a woman called "Babylon the great", drunk with the blood of the martyrs and seated on seven mountains, "the great city, which reigns over the kings of the earth" [[bible:Rev 6:9-11]] [[bible:Rev 13:7-8]] [[bible:Rev 17:5-9, 18]]. When [[person:irenaeus]] discussed the beast's number, one of the names he weighed was *Lateinos*, because "the Latins are they who at present bear rule"; he would not decide between it and others [[cite:irenaeus-date]]. The book ends with a new heaven and a new earth, the new Jerusalem coming down from God, and the prayer "Yes, come, Lord Jesus" [[bible:Rev 21:1-4]] [[bible:Rev 22:20]].

The writer names himself only as [[person:john-of-patmos|John]] and counts himself among the prophets [[bible:Rev 22:8-9]]. [[person:justin-martyr|Justin]], in the middle of the second century, calls him "one of the apostles of Christ" [[cite:justin]], and Irenaeus calls him John, the Lord's disciple [[cite:irenaeus-john]]. In the third century Dionysius of Alexandria argued from its language and style that the book was not by the author of John's Gospel, though he held it to be the work of a holy man of that name [[cite:dionysius]]. [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]] suggested a second John, an elder mentioned by Papias [[cite:elder]]. The identification with the apostle is therefore ancient, and so is the doubt.

Eusebius reports as tradition that John was banished to Patmos under [[person:domitian]] and returned to Ephesus when Domitian died [[cite:exile]] [[cite:return]]. He also records that in his own day some received the book as Scripture and others rejected it [[cite:canon]].
