  Josephus, who was there, dates both ends. The war began in the month Artemisius of the twelfth year of Nero, the spring of AD 66 [[cite:outbreak]]. The temple burned on the tenth of the month Loos and the last part of the city fell on the eighth of Gorpiaeus, in the second year of Vespasian [[cite:burning]] [[cite:year]]. Vespasian's reign was counted from 1 July 69 [[cite:accession]], so the year is 70.

  Josephus uses Macedonian month names for the Jewish months, and their exact equivalents in the Roman calendar are not agreed. The burning of the temple is usually put at the end of August and the fall of the upper city about a month later. Josephus notes that the tenth of Loos was the day on which the Babylonians had burned the first temple [[cite:burning]] [[bible:Jer 52:12-13]].

[[person:josephus]] puts the outbreak in the spring of AD 66, under the governor Gessius Florus, whose greed and violence he blames for it [[cite:outbreak]]. In the temple its captain, Eleazar, persuaded the priests to accept no offering from a foreigner, and the sacrifice for the emperor stopped. Josephus calls this the true beginning of the war [[cite:sacrifice]]. Cestius Gallus, the governor of Syria, intervened with an army and was defeated with the loss of more than five thousand men [[cite:cestius]].

[[person:nero]] gave the command to [[person:vespasian]], who gathered an army in Syria with his son [[person:titus]] [[cite:command]]. Josephus himself, the rebel commander in Galilee [[cite:galilee]], was captured at Jotapata and, by his own account, told Vespasian that he would be emperor [[cite:jotapata]]. Tacitus says that in two summers Vespasian took everything except [[place:jerusalem]], and that the civil war at [[place:rome]] then halted the campaign [[cite:tacitus-war]]. In July 69 the legions of Egypt and Judaea proclaimed Vespasian emperor [[cite:accession]], and the war was left to Titus [[cite:tacitus-war]].

Titus encamped before the city at Passover in AD 70, when it was full of pilgrims [[cite:camp]] [[cite:numbers]]. Inside, rival factions had been fighting one another, and famine set in [[cite:factions]] [[cite:numbers]]. On the seventeenth of Panemus the daily sacrifice ceased for want of men to offer it [[cite:daily]]. On the tenth of Loos the temple burned. Josephus says that a soldier threw a brand through a window without orders and that Titus tried in vain to have the fire put out [[cite:burning]]. The rest of the city fell a month later [[cite:fall]], and Titus had the city and the temple levelled, leaving three towers and part of the western wall [[cite:razed]]. Josephus counts 1,100,000 dead and 97,000 prisoners [[cite:numbers]]; Tacitus had heard that the besieged numbered 600,000 [[cite:factions]].

At the triumph in Rome the golden table, the seven-branched lampstand and a copy of the Law were carried in procession [[cite:triumph]]. The relief on the Arch of Titus shows soldiers bearing the lampstand, the table and the trumpets [[cite:arch]]. The tax that Jews everywhere had paid to the temple now went to the Capitol in Rome [[cite:tax]].

Josephus is the main narrative source. He watched the siege from the Roman camp and afterwards lived at Rome under the patronage of the victors [[cite:life]]; he blames the rebels for the city's ruin and clears Titus of burning the temple [[cite:fall]] [[cite:burning]]. The surviving text of Tacitus breaks off before the city falls.

The Gospels record a saying of Jesus that not one stone of the temple would be left on another, and Luke speaks of Jerusalem surrounded by armies [[bible:Mark 13:1-2]] [[bible:Luke 21:20-24]]. [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]], some 250 years after the war, reports that the Christians of Jerusalem, warned by a revelation given before the war, left the city and settled at Pella in Perea [[cite:pella]].
