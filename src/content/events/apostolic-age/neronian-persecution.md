  Tacitus tells of the fire under the consuls Gaius Laecanius and Marcus Licinius, the year AD 64 [[cite:year]], and says that it broke out on 19 July [[cite:fire]]. The year of the fire is not in doubt.

  The punishment of the Christians is less exactly placed. Tacitus sets it after the first measures of rebuilding and the religious rites that followed the fire, without a date [[cite:christians]]; it is usually put in the later part of 64. Suetonius gives no date at all [[cite:suetonius-christians]].

  Of the writers cited here only Tacitus links the Christians with the fire, and he wrote some fifty years later. Brent D. Shaw, "The Myth of the Neronian Persecution", *Journal of Roman Studies* 105 (2015), argues that the passage reflects the ideas of Tacitus's own day and not the events of the 60s. Christopher P. Jones, "The Historicity of the Neronian Persecution: A Response to Brent Shaw", *New Testament Studies* 63 (2017), defends the account as credible. Most historians accept it in substance.

The fire began on 19 July among the shops beside the Circus Maximus and burned for six days, then broke out a second time. Of the fourteen districts of [[place:rome]], Tacitus says, four were untouched and three were levelled. [[person:nero]] was at Antium when it started. He returned, opened the Campus Martius and his own gardens to the homeless and lowered the price of grain, but a report spread that he had sung of the fall of Troy while the city burned [[cite:fire]]. Tacitus leaves open whether the fire was an accident or the emperor's doing [[cite:fire]]. Suetonius and Cassius Dio say outright that Nero set it [[cite:suetonius-fire]] [[cite:dio]].

What followed is told at length by Tacitus. Neither relief nor religious rites, he says, could end the belief that the fire had been ordered. To stop the rumour Nero put the blame on a group "called Christians by the populace". Tacitus explains the name: Christus had been executed by the procurator [[person:pontius-pilate|Pontius Pilatus]] in the reign of [[person:tiberius]], and the movement had spread from Judaea to the capital. First those who confessed were arrested. On their information a great number were then convicted, less for arson than for "hatred against mankind". Some were dressed in animal skins and torn by dogs, some were crucified, and some were burned as torches after dark. Nero gave his gardens for the spectacle and went among the crowd dressed as a charioteer. Tacitus thinks the Christians deserved punishment, yet he records that people pitied them, because they seemed to die for one man's cruelty and not for the public good [[cite:christians]].

Suetonius confirms the punishment and not the occasion. In a list of Nero's public measures he says that penalties were inflicted on the Christians, "a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition" [[cite:suetonius-christians]]. He describes the fire in another place and does not connect the two [[cite:suetonius-fire]]. Dio's account of the fire does not mention Christians [[cite:dio]].

A Christian letter from Rome, a generation later, may refer to these deaths. After speaking of [[person:peter]] and [[person:paul]], the letter known as 1 Clement adds that a "great multitude" of believers suffered insults and tortures, among them women [[cite:clement]]. It names neither Nero nor the fire. By about AD 200 [[person:tertullian]], in words that [[person:eusebius-of-caesarea|Eusebius]] quotes, called Nero the first emperor to persecute the Christians [[cite:tertullian]].

The sources cited here give no number of the dead, mention no law against the Christians, and speak only of the city of Rome. Later tradition placed the deaths of Peter and Paul in this persecution; see [[event:martyrdom-of-peter-and-paul]].
