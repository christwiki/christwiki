dating: |
  The stele dates itself to the third day of the third month of the third season in the fifth year of Merneptah [[cite:date]]. Turning that into a calendar year depends on the reconstruction of Egyptian chronology. On the one now most used, Ramesses II reigned for 66 years, about 1279–1213 BC, and Merneptah about 1213–1203 BC, so that year 5 falls about 1209 or 1208 BC. The reign dates are those given by James K. Hoffmeier, "What Is the Biblical Date for the Exodus? A Response to Bryant Wood", *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 50 (2007), p. 232, who puts the campaign about 1208 BC (p. 243). Other reconstructions move the reign by some years; the older "high" chronology put Ramesses II at 1304–1237 BC (Hoffmeier, p. 239). The stele dates the Libyan victory, not the fighting in Canaan, which is mentioned only in passing.

  The writing of the name, which marks Israel as a people and not as a city or a land, is studied by Michael G. Hasel, "Israel in the Merneptah Stela", *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* 296 (1994), pp. 45–61. J. H. Breasted showed that "his seed is not" is a set phrase used of other defeated enemies (*Ancient Records of Egypt*, vol. 3, 1906, §§ 603–605). A reading of "Israel" on an older inscribed block in Berlin, proposed by Manfred Görg, is disputed; Hoffmeier rejects it (pp. 241–242).

[[person:merneptah]], the son and successor of Ramesses II [[cite:record]], faced an invasion of Egypt from Libya in the fifth year of his reign and turned it back. A hymn celebrating the victory [[cite:hymn]] was carved on a granite stele [[cite:record]] more than three metres high [[cite:size]]. The stone was an old one. It had been made for Amenhotep III, and Merneptah's text was cut on its back [[cite:record]]. It stood in the first court of Merneptah's mortuary temple on the west bank at [[place:thebes]], where Flinders Petrie found it fallen on its face in 1896 [[cite:find]].

Almost all of the twenty-eight lines are about Libya [[cite:hymn]]. The closing lines turn to the rest of Egypt's neighbours. No one among the foreign peoples lifts his head: Libya is wasted and the Hittite land is at peace; Canaan is plundered, Ashkelon carried off and Gezer seized [[cite:strophe]]. Yanoam is "made as a thing not existing", and then comes the line that gives the stele its modern name: "Israel is desolated, his seed is not" [[cite:israel]].

The statement is short, and it is a boast. What it shows is limited but firm. A group called Israel existed in Merneptah's fifth year. It was in or near Canaan, since it is listed there with Ashkelon, Gezer and Yanoam. And, as the museum's record notes, the name refers to a people and not to a state [[cite:record]]; Wilhelm Spiegelberg, who first published the text, rendered it "the people of Israel" [[cite:spiegelberg]]. The stele says nothing of where this people came from. It does not mention Moses, a stay in Egypt or an exodus.

No undisputed older mention of Israel outside the Bible is known. For the dispute about the date of [[event:the-exodus]] the stele is the one fixed point: on any reckoning Israel has to be in or near Canaan by about 1208 BC. Beyond that it has been read in several ways from the start. Petrie, publishing the find, set out five different views of how these Israelites might relate to those of the Bible [[cite:petrie-note]].
