  "Emperor" is a later word for his position. Augustus held no office of that name. He describes himself as *princeps*, the leading citizen [[cite:janus]], and counts his powers by his consulships and his years of tribunician power [[cite:offices]]. Historians date the beginning of rule by emperors from 27 BC because the arrangements made then lasted; Dio, looking back, says so in as many words [[cite:monarchy]].

[[person:augustus|Gaius Octavius]] was the great-nephew of Julius Caesar and took Caesar's name under his will [[cite:plancus]]. After Caesar's murder he raised an army at the age of nineteen, became one of three men appointed to reorder the state, and defeated Caesar's assassins [[cite:youth]]. In 31 BC Octavian, as he is called for these years, defeated his last rival, Mark Antony, in the sea battle off Actium. He then took [[place:alexandria]], where Antony had fled, and added Egypt to Rome's empire [[cite:actium]] [[cite:actium-suetonius]].

What he did with that power he describes himself, in the record of his deeds that was inscribed after his death. "In my sixth and seventh consulships, when I had extinguished the flames of civil war, after receiving by universal consent the absolute control of affairs," he writes, "I transferred the republic from my own control to the will of the senate and the Roman people." In return the senate gave him the name Augustus. From then on, he says, he excelled all others in rank, but had no more power than his colleagues in each office [[cite:settlement]].

Cassius Dio, a senator writing more than two centuries later, tells the same events less kindly. Octavian came into the senate in [[place:rome]] and read a speech laying down his powers. The senators, some sincerely and some from fear, begged him to keep them [[cite:speech]]. He then accepted for ten years the provinces that needed armies, and left the peaceful ones to the senate, so that he alone commanded soldiers [[cite:provinces]]. Dio says that he would have liked the name Romulus but saw that it suggested kingship, and took "Augustus", a word used of sacred things [[cite:name]]. Suetonius names the senator who proposed it, Munatius Plancus [[cite:plancus]]. Dio's verdict is that the power of people and senate now passed wholly to one man and that Rome, whatever it was called, was from this time a monarchy [[cite:monarchy]].

Augustus ruled until his death in AD 14 [[cite:death]]. He records that the gates of Janus, shut only in time of peace, were closed three times while he was leading citizen [[cite:janus]]. [[person:herod-the-great|Herod]] of Judea kept his throne under him; Tacitus says that Augustus, after his victory, increased Herod's power [[cite:herod]].

This is the government under which the New Testament opens. Luke dates the birth of [[person:jesus]] by a decree of "Caesar Augustus" that all the world should be enrolled [[bible:Luke 2:1]], and the ministry of [[person:john-the-baptist]] by the fifteenth year of his successor [[person:tiberius]] [[bible:Luke 3:1]]. Jesus is asked whether it is lawful to pay taxes to Caesar [[bible:Mark 12:14-17]], and at his trial the chief priests tell [[person:pontius-pilate|Pilate]], "We have no king but Caesar!" [[bible:John 19:15]].
