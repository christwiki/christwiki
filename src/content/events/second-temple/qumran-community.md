dating: |
  None of the community's own writings gives a date. The Damascus Document places its beginnings 390 years after Nebuchadnezzar took Jerusalem, followed by twenty years before the Teacher of Righteousness appeared [[cite:teacher]]. Counted literally that leads to the early second century BC, but the figure repeats Ezekiel's 390 years [[bible:Ezek 4:5]] and is often read as symbolic. The earliest setting in which Josephus mentions the Essenes is his account of the 140s BC [[cite:sects]]. The Cave 1 copy of the Community Rule was written about 100–75 BC [[cite:rule]], and the scrolls as a whole range from the third century BC to the first century AD [[cite:scrolls]].

  The dates of the settlement at Qumran come from excavation. Roland de Vaux, who dug the site in 1951–1956, put the arrival of the community in the later second century BC and its end in AD 68: *Archaeology and the Dead Sea Scrolls* (1973). Jodi Magness, *The Archaeology of Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls* (2002), brings the beginning down to about 100–50 BC. The end is tied to the Roman campaign of AD 68, when Josephus has Vespasian at Jericho and on the shore of the Dead Sea [[cite:vespasian]].

  That the people of the scrolls were Essenes is the majority view, argued for example by James C. VanderKam, *The Dead Sea Scrolls Today* (2nd ed., 2010). Lawrence H. Schiffman, *Reclaiming the Dead Sea Scrolls* (1994), stresses the Sadducean character of their law, and Norman Golb, *Who Wrote the Dead Sea Scrolls?* (1995), holds that the scrolls came from libraries in Jerusalem and had no link with the site.

Between 1947 and 1956 the remains of hundreds of manuscripts were found in eleven caves in the cliffs near [[place:qumran]], on the north-west shore of the Dead Sea [[cite:caves]]. They date from the third century BC to the first century AD. Some are copies of the books of the Hebrew Bible. Others are Jewish religious writings of the period, and about a quarter of these seem to be the work of one community: its rules, its commentaries on Scripture and its hopes for the end of the age [[cite:scrolls]].

The Community Rule is that group's own handbook. It lays down how members were admitted, how they ate together and how offences were punished. It teaches that humanity is divided between the righteous and the wicked and that all things are predestined [[cite:rule]] [[cite:rule-4q258]]. One passage, in its rules for the community's instructor, speaks of a time for "clearing the way" in the wilderness, which the translator takes to mean the study of the law [[cite:wilderness]]. The words echo Isaiah's call to prepare the way of the Lord in the wilderness [[bible:Isa 40:3]], the verse the Gospels apply to [[person:john-the-baptist]] [[bible:Mark 1:2-4]].

The Damascus Document tells how the movement began. In the "end of the wrath", it says, 390 years after [[person:nebuchadnezzar-ii|Nebuchadnezzar]], God caused a root to grow from Israel and Aaron. For twenty years they groped like blind men, until God raised up for them "a teacher of righteousness" [[cite:teacher]]. It speaks of those who went out from the land of Judah and entered "the New Covenant in the land of Damascus" [[cite:covenant]], and it makes rules for members who live in camps, marry and have children [[cite:camps]].

Greek and Roman readers knew of such a group as the Essenes. [[person:josephus]], who names them beside the Pharisees and Sadducees, describes men who held their property in common, bathed before a common meal, kept the sabbath with great strictness and admitted new members only after three years of testing. Most did not marry, and they lived in many towns [[cite:essenes]]. He counts about four thousand of them [[cite:number]]. Pliny the Elder, writing in the AD 70s, places a settlement of Essenes west of the Dead Sea, with En-gedi "below" them [[cite:pliny]]. That the people of the scrolls were Essenes is an inference from these descriptions, widely but not universally accepted [[cite:scrolls]]; see the dating note.

The scrolls contain copies of the books of the Hebrew Bible more than a thousand years older than those known before [[cite:scrolls]], among them an almost complete Isaiah of about 125 BC [[cite:isaiah]]. And they show, from its own writings, a Jewish movement that was still active in the lifetime of [[person:jesus]] and that spoke, as the New Testament does, of preparing the way in the wilderness and of a new covenant [[bible:Jer 31:31]] [[bible:Luke 22:20]]. No Christian writing was found among the scrolls [[cite:background]].
