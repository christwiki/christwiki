dating: |
  Josephus dates the fall of the temple mount to the 179th Olympiad and the consulship of Gaius Antonius and Marcus Tullius Cicero, which is 63 BC [[cite:date]]. Cicero, the consul of that year, refers to the capture four years later as a known fact [[cite:cicero]].

  The time of year is uncertain. Josephus says that the city fell "on the third month, on the day of the fast" [[cite:date]]; in the *War* the third month is the third month of the siege [[cite:war]]. Cassius Dio says that it fell on "the day of Saturn", the sabbath [[cite:dio]]. Some take the fast to be the Day of Atonement, in the autumn. Others think that Josephus's source meant the sabbath, which Greek and Roman writers wrongly supposed to be a fast day, and put the capture in the summer: see Emil Schürer, *The History of the Jewish People in the Age of Jesus Christ*, revised by G. Vermes and F. Millar, vol. 1 (1973), section 12.

  Josephus wrote about 140 to 160 years after the event and used earlier historians, among whom he names Strabo, Nicolaus of Damascus and Livy [[cite:siege]]. The Psalms of Solomon are the reaction of Jews in Jerusalem within a few years. They do not name Pompey, but the conqueror they describe is left dead and unburied in Egypt [[cite:psalm2]], as Pompey was, and almost all scholars take him to be Pompey: see Kenneth Atkinson, *I Cried to the Lord* (2004).

After the death of the Hasmonean queen Alexandra her two sons fought for power. The elder, [[person:hyrcanus-ii]], was high priest. The younger, [[person:aristobulus-ii]], defeated him and took the kingship. Antipater, an Idumean and the father of the future king [[person:herod-the-great|Herod]], persuaded Hyrcanus to call in the Arab king Aretas, who besieged Aristobulus in the temple at [[place:jerusalem]] [[cite:brothers]]. A Roman officer in Syria took Aristobulus's money and ordered Aretas off [[cite:scaurus]].

In the spring of 63 BC [[person:pompey]], Rome's commander in the East, heard both brothers at [[place:damascus]], and with them a delegation of the nation that wanted neither and asked to be governed by priests, not kings. He put off his decision, and Aristobulus left to prepare for war [[cite:damascus]]. Pompey marched on Jerusalem. When Aristobulus's soldiers refused to hand over the city, he arrested Aristobulus [[cite:march]].

Hyrcanus's supporters opened the gates. Those of Aristobulus held the temple mount and cut its bridge to the city. The Romans filled the ravine on the north side and raised a ramp, working on sabbaths, when the defenders would not go out to stop them. In the third month a tower fell and they broke in. [[person:josephus]] says that the priests went on with the sacrifices and were cut down at the altar, and that twelve thousand Jews died [[cite:siege]] [[cite:war]].

Pompey and his officers went into the sanctuary, where only the high priest might enter. Josephus says that he touched nothing, not even the two thousand talents of sacred money, and the next day ordered the temple cleansed and the sacrifices resumed [[cite:sanctuary]]. Cicero, speaking at [[place:rome]] four years later, agrees that Pompey took nothing [[cite:cicero]]. Cassius Dio, more than 250 years afterwards, says that the wealth was plundered [[cite:dio]]. Tacitus notes that the inner shrine was now known to be empty, with no image [[cite:tacitus]]. Pompey restored Hyrcanus as high priest and head of the nation, without a crown [[cite:diadem]]. He imposed tribute, put the coastal and Greek cities that the Hasmoneans had conquered under the governor of Syria, and took Aristobulus and his children to Rome. "We lost our liberty," Josephus writes, "and became subject to the Romans" [[cite:settlement]].

The Psalms of Solomon give the response of pious Jews in the city. They tell of a sinner who broke down the walls with a battering-ram while foreigners trampled the altar [[cite:psalm2]], and of leaders who went out to welcome him and opened the gates [[cite:psalm8]]. They read the disaster as judgment on Jerusalem's own sins, and they pray for a king, the son of David, "the anointed of the Lord", to cleanse the city of the nations that trample it [[cite:psalm17]]. From this year Judea lay under Roman power; see [[event:herod-becomes-king]].
