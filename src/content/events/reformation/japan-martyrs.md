  Hideyoshi's edicts carry the dates of the eighteenth and nineteenth days of the sixth month of the fifteenth year of Tenshō, which is 1587 [[cite:edicts]]. Morga gives 5 February 1597, St Agatha's day, for the crucifixions [[cite:morga-crucifixion]]; Fróis says that the prisoners reached a place eight or nine leagues from Nagasaki on 4 February and were put to death the next day, the feast of St Agatha [[cite:frois-date]]. Ernest Satow, who translated the proclamation against Christianity, says that it was published on 27 January 1614, "in the 12th month of the 18th year of Keichō", and heads it "Proclamation of Iyeyasu" [[cite:edict-1614]]. He names no day of the Japanese month. Nathalie Kouamé, "Sûden's Anti-Christian Edict (The) (1614)", in *Encyclopédie des historiographies: Afriques, Amériques, Asies*, vol. 1 (2020), pp. 1760–1779, reports from the register of foreign affairs kept by the Zen monk Ishin Sūden that Ieyasu gave him the order on the evening of the 22nd day of that month, which she converts to 31 January 1614, that Sūden wrote the text that night, and that it was signed by the shogun Hidetada, Ieyasu's son; the text as she prints it is dated only to the twelfth month. On her conversion Satow's 27 January would be the 18th day of the month, before the text was written. The two cannot both be right, and hers rests on the drafter's own register; the narrative therefore gives the month and reports Satow's day as his. James Murdoch, *A History of Japan*, vol. 2 (1903), p. 611, says that the proclamation appeared under the name of Ieyasu's son Hidetada; at pp. 499–503 he follows the expulsion of the missionaries through 1614, drawing on the Jesuit annual letter of that year.

  Satow adds that the proclamation "was accompanied by a set of fifteen rules" for the priests who were to guarantee the orthodoxy of their parishioners, and prints them; they name the "Fujiu-fuze" and the "Hiden-sect" beside the Christians [[cite:edict-rules]]. Tamamuro Fumio, "Local Society and the Temple-Parishioner Relationship within the Bakufu's Governance Structure", *Japanese Journal of Religious Studies* 28/3–4 (2001), pp. 265–266, identifies this document, which carries the date of the fifth month of 1613, as a forgery made no earlier than about 1735, because it names the Fuju-fuse and Hiden groups, banned only in 1669 and 1691, and presupposes a rule of 1729. He dates the order that every Japanese hold a certificate of temple registration to 1638 (p. 262) and says that in 1614 such certificates were required only of Christians who had been made to give up their faith (p. 266). The narrative therefore says nothing of the rules.

In 1587 [[person:toyotomi-hideyoshi]], ruler of Japan, issued two edicts on Christianity. The first forbade lords to force the people of their domains to convert. The second called Japan the country of the gods, accused the missionaries of having shrines and temples destroyed, and ordered them to leave within twenty days, while the foreign ships might go on trading [[cite:edicts]]. The missionaries did not all leave. Ten years later Jesuits were established in the country, and Franciscans from the Philippines kept a house and hospital in the capital with Hideyoshi's sufferance [[cite:morga-wreck]].

In 1596 the Spanish galleon *San Felipe*, sailing from Manila for Mexico, lost its rudder in storms and ran aground on the Japanese coast, and Hideyoshi seized its cargo. Antonio de Morga, an official at Manila, reports that the ship's pilot, showing a Japanese official a chart of the Spanish king's dominions, said that the friars went in first to preach and the soldiers followed, and that this was repeated to Hideyoshi [[cite:morga-wreck]]. He ordered the friars of the capital and their Japanese helpers to be crucified [[cite:morga-order]]. The sentence carried before them said that they had come from the Philippines as ambassadors and stayed "preaching the Christian religion, which in former years I have strictly forbidden" [[cite:morga-sentence]].

They were taken to [[place:nagasaki]] and crucified on a hill in sight of the town on 5 February 1597 [[cite:morga-crucifixion]]. The Jesuit Luís Fróis, writing that year, counts twenty-six: six Franciscans in the middle and ten Japanese on either side, three of whom belonged to the Society of Jesus. They were bound to the crosses and killed with lances. One of the three, the Japanese brother [[person:paul-miki]], spoke from his cross. He said that he was a Japanese and a Jesuit and was dying for having preached the gospel, that there was no way to salvation but the one the Christians hold, and that he forgave the ruler and all who had a part in his death and asked them to seek baptism [[cite:frois]]. The sentence and the friar's letter that Morga prints speak of twenty-four [[cite:morga-sentence]] [[cite:morga-letter]].

In the twelfth month of the eighteenth year of Keichō, early in 1614, the government of [[person:tokugawa-ieyasu]] issued a proclamation ordering Christianity suppressed; its translator gives the day as 27 January [[cite:edict-1614]]. It calls Japan "the country of gods and of Buddha". The Christians, it says, have come "longing to disseminate an evil law" so as to "change the government of the country, and obtain possession of the land"; they honour condemned criminals; and they are to be swept out until "not an inch of soil remains to them in Japan" [[cite:edict-1614]].
