On 25 February 1570 [[person:pius-v]] issued at [[place:rome]] the bull *Regnans in excelsis*. It declared [[person:elizabeth-i]] a heretic, pronounced her deprived of her "pretended title" to the crown and commanded her subjects not to obey her [[cite:regnans]]. William Camden, whose history was printed forty-five years later, says that John Felton set the bull up on the gates of the bishop of London's palace and was hanged for it, and that most of the more moderate Catholics secretly disliked the bull [[cite:camden]]. In 1571 Parliament made it high treason to use a bull from Rome or to be reconciled to Rome by one [[cite:bulls]]; that law and those that followed it are told under [[event:elizabethan-settlement|the Elizabethan settlement]].

In June 1580 the Jesuit [[person:edmund-campion]] landed at Dover [[cite:allen-arrival]]. In a paper for the Privy Council he wrote that he was a priest sent to preach the gospel and minister the sacraments, "strictly forbidden" to deal with matters of state, and he asked to be heard before the Council, the universities and the lawyers [[cite:challenge]]. The government later printed a document taken, it said, from an accomplice after Campion's death: on 14 April 1580 the pope had granted Campion and Robert Persons an explanation that the bull always bound the queen and the heretics but did not bind Catholics as things stood, only when it could be publicly executed [[cite:cecil-faculties]].

By the account of the Catholic writer William Allen, Campion was taken on 17 July 1581 [[cite:allen-arrest]], lodged in the Tower in [[place:london]] and racked [[cite:allen-tower]]. In November he and others were indicted for having plotted abroad the queen's death and an invasion, and a jury found them guilty [[cite:allen-trial]]. On 1 December he was drawn to Tyburn with two other priests [[cite:allen-tyburn]]. There, Allen reports, he denied the treasons charged, but "if you esteeme my religion treason" he must grant it; asked his opinion of the bull, he gave no answer; and he prayed for Elizabeth, "your queene and my queene" [[cite:allen-scaffold]].

The government's defence, *The Execution of Justice in England*, was penned, its 1675 editor says, by direction of Lord Treasurer Burghley [[cite:cecil-preface]]. It says that the seminary priests and Jesuits came to persuade the people to obey the bull and were condemned as traitors under the treason law of Edward III, "not being dealt withal upon questions of Religion" [[cite:cecil-case]]. By the Catholics' own catalogue, it adds, not above sixty had been executed in twenty-five years, against almost four hundred who died under [[person:mary-i-of-england]] [[cite:cecil-numbers]]. Allen answered that many had died "for mere matter of religion" under new statutes that made cases of conscience treason [[cite:defence-religion]], and that Campion and those arraigned with him under the old statute were not guilty [[cite:defence-campion]].

In 1970 Pope Paul VI canonised forty martyrs of England and Wales, Campion among them. They had been, he said, faithful subjects of the royal power, which they recognised as legitimate in all that belongs to the civil and political order, and their loyalty to it came into conflict with their fidelity to God [[cite:paul-vi]].
