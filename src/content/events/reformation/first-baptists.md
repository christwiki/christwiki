In a book printed in 1609 [[person:john-smyth]] described his own course as a passage from Puritanism to "Brownism", the Separatists' rejection of the Church of England as a false church, and from there to "true Christian baptism" [[cite:epistle]]. The title page states two propositions: that infants are not to be baptized, and that converts from "Antichristian" churches are to be admitted to the true church by baptism [[cite:title]]. Anyone who rejects the Church of England as false, he wrote, must reject the baptism it gave as well [[cite:epistle]].

His congregation, which [[person:william-bradford]] places at [[place:amsterdam]] [[cite:bradford-amsterdam]], therefore began again. [[person:john-robinson]], pastor of another English Separatist congregation abroad, wrote in 1614 that he had heard from members of the group how it was done: they dissolved their former church, and Smyth baptized first himself, then [[person:thomas-helwys]], then the rest, each making a confession [[cite:robinson]]. Smyth defended the act against the charge that no one may baptize himself: if two or three may form a church where there is none, he argued, they may also recover baptism [[cite:self-baptism]].

The group divided. Smyth came to hold that baptism should be sought from a true church where one exists; he wrote that they had baptized themselves only because they then knew no church they could join with a good conscience, and that Helwys accused him of sinning against the Holy Spirit by abandoning what he had once acknowledged [[cite:last-book]].

In 1611 a confession of twenty-seven articles was printed in the name of the "English people remaining at Amsterdam"; its editor attributes it to Helwys [[cite:declaration-title]]. It defines the church as a company of faithful people joined to the Lord and to one another by baptism on their own confession of the faith and of sins, and says that baptism "in no wise" belongs to infants [[cite:declaration-church]]. It teaches that God would have all people saved and that believers may fall from grace [[cite:declaration-grace]]. It calls magistracy a holy ordinance of God, allows magistrates to be church members, and charges Smyth with denying this [[cite:declaration-magistrate]]. The Swiss Brethren of the previous century had held that a Christian may not serve as a magistrate [[cite:schleitheim]]; see [[event:anabaptists]].

In 1612, in the reign of [[person:james-vi-and-i|James I]], Helwys published a book that speaks directly to "our lord the King" [[cite:mystery-title]] [[cite:conscience]]. He argued that those who had fled persecution should come back to their own country [[cite:flight]], and in 1614 Robinson wrote of him and his people as being in [[place:london]] [[cite:robinson-london]]. The king, Helwys wrote, has authority in earthly matters only and none over consciences, those of Roman Catholics included: "Let them be heretikes, Turcks, Jewes, or whatsoever it apperteynes not to the earthly power to punish them in the least measure" [[cite:conscience]].
