In 1516 the press of Johann Froben at [[place:basel]] issued a large volume with the title *Novum Instrumentum omne*, the whole New Testament, which the title page says was carefully revised and corrected by [[person:erasmus]] of Rotterdam [[cite:title]]. The last leaf dates the printing to February 1516 [[cite:colophon]]. On 7 March Erasmus told a correspondent that the New Testament was published [[cite:regius]].

The book prints the Greek text and a Latin version side by side in two columns [[cite:john]]. The title page says that the text has been corrected against the Greek, against many manuscripts in both languages and against the quotations of the fathers, among them [[person:origen]], [[person:john-chrysostom|Chrysostom]], Cyril, [[person:jerome]], [[person:cyprian]], [[person:ambrose]], Hilary and [[person:augustine]], and that notes tell the reader what has been changed and why. It asks the reader who meets a change to weigh whether it is for the better before taking offence [[cite:title]].

Erasmus dedicated the work to Pope [[person:leo-x]]. He writes that he has put it out under the pope's name so that its usefulness may spread more widely [[cite:dedication]]. That August he wrote to Leo again and stated his aim: not to tear up "the old and commonly accepted edition" but to emend it where it was corrupt and make it clear where it was obscure, on the evidence of the earliest manuscripts and of fathers approved by the church. He added that he was ready to correct any error of his own [[cite:leo]].

Before the text stands an exhortation to the reader, the *Paraclesis*. Erasmus says there that he disagrees with those who would not have Scripture translated into all tongues and read by lay men and women. He wishes that all women would read the Gospel and Paul's letters, and, in the English version of 1529, "I would to God, the plowman would sing a text of the scripture at his plowbeam, and that the weaver at his loom, with this would drive away the tediousness of time" [[cite:paraclesis]].

Erasmus admitted that the work had been hurried. In June 1516 he wrote to a friend in England that it had come out "fairly accurate, considering the limited time" and that he would soon be at work on another edition [[cite:latimer]].

The book set two things before its readers together: a New Testament corrected from Greek manuscripts [[cite:title]] and the wish that everyone should read it in their own language [[cite:paraclesis]]. [[person:philip-melanchthon]] later wrote that the students at [[place:wittenberg]] were at this time directed to the writings of Erasmus for Latin and Greek, and that [[person:martin-luther]] took up Greek and Hebrew [[cite:melanchthon]]. The German and English New Testaments of the next ten years are told under [[event:luther-bible]] and [[event:tyndale-new-testament]].
