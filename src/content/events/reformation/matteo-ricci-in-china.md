  The Latin edition of Ricci's own account gives the day: the party reached Beijing on 24 January 1601, as the Chinese year was ending [[cite:arrival]]. Pantoja's letter gives no day: it says that they were held under guard for more than two and a half months of the winter and travelled four days to the capital once the emperor sent for them, and a marginal note in Purchas's printing puts the end of the confinement at the beginning of January 1601 [[cite:held]]. A petition of 1610 translated in the same book dates the arrival to the twelfth moon of the twenty-eighth year of Wanli and Ricci's death to the eighteenth day of the third moon of the thirty-eighth year [[cite:burial]], ten years later, which the narrative gives as 11 May [[cite:death]].

In 1602 the Jesuit Diego de Pantoja, who had entered China from the Portuguese town of [[place:macau]], wrote from [[place:beijing]] that in twenty years no more than five or six priests of his order had got into the country, and that they had long looked for a way to reach the emperor with a gift [[cite:entry]]. In May 1600 [[person:matteo-ricci]], Pantoja and a Chinese Jesuit brother left Nanjing by boat with two clocks, three oil paintings of Christ and of the Virgin, and a finely bound breviary [[cite:gifts]]. A eunuch who collected the emperor's tolls detained them on the way and took some of their goods [[cite:eunuch]].

After six months the emperor asked for the clocks, and the party reached Beijing on 24 January 1601 [[cite:arrival]]. Pantoja reports that the emperor was delighted with the paintings and clocks and had four eunuchs taught to tend them, but did not receive the Jesuits himself and had their portraits painted instead [[cite:court]]. The official responsible for foreigners held them under guard for about three months and petitioned for them to be sent away; the emperor did not answer, and they rented a house and kept an allowance from the court [[cite:house]].

Ricci wrote a catechism in Chinese meant chiefly for readers outside the church; the Jesuit account says that it argued from natural reason more than from Scripture, quoted the ancient Chinese books, and refuted the sects of China except the school of Confucius [[cite:catechism]]. The same account says that the yearly offerings to ancestors seem free of idolatry and "perhaps" of superstition, and adds that Christians would do better to give alms instead [[cite:rites]].

It also tells of the Chinese scholars who joined the Jesuits. [[person:xu-guangqi]] of [[place:shanghai|Shanghai]] visited the Jesuit house at Nanjing in 1603, read the catechism in manuscript through the night and was baptized, as Paul, before he went home [[cite:xu-baptism]]. The next year he took the highest degree at Beijing [[cite:xu-doctor]], and he urged that a book of European science be published [[cite:euclid]]. Ricci's preface to their Chinese Euclid, dated 1607, says that he explained the text aloud while Xu wrote it down [[cite:ricci-preface]]. Xu's preface calls mathematics the least part of Ricci's learning, the greatest being to cultivate the self and serve Heaven, and says he publishes the lesser first because it is easier to believe [[cite:xu-preface]]. In 1616, when the Board of Rites accused the Jesuits, he told the emperor in a memorial that he was one of those who believed their books [[cite:xu-memorial]].

Ricci died in Beijing on 11 May 1610 [[cite:death]], and the emperor granted land for his grave [[cite:burial]]. For the later dispute over these rites, see [[event:chinese-rites-controversy]].
