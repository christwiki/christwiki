dating: |
  St Bartholomew's Day is 24 August. A letter written in Paris on 22 August 1572 reports the shooting of Coligny that day [[cite:csp-538]], a royal lieutenant's proclamation of 28 August already speaks of the slaughter in Paris [[cite:csp-545]], and in October the Parlement of Paris ordered a thanksgiving on every anniversary of St Bartholomew [[cite:csp-622]]. The killings in the provinces ran on into September [[cite:csp-583]].

  Estimates of the number killed differ very widely. Philip Benedict, "The Saint Bartholomew's Massacres in the Provinces", *The Historical Journal* 21 (1978), pp. 205–225, calls the casualty figures "notoriously unreliable" and arrives at a rough figure of about 3,000 for the provincial towns taken together. For Paris this entry reports only what an English informant heard at the time.

Marguerite de Valois, the Catholic sister of King [[person:charles-ix-of-france]], was married at Notre-Dame in [[place:paris]] to the Protestant king of Navarre, the later [[person:henry-iv-of-france]], who had come to the city with eight hundred gentlemen [[cite:marguerite-4]]. Among the French Protestants, the Huguenots, in Paris in August 1572 was Admiral [[person:gaspard-de-coligny]] [[cite:csp-538]].

On 22 August Coligny was shot from a window as he walked back from the court to his lodging and was wounded in the hand and arm. An Englishman who saw him in bed that day wrote that the king took it "very grievously" [[cite:csp-538]]. Marguerite, writing many years later, puts the wound in the shoulder. She says that the king blamed the duke of Guise and meant to punish him, and was then told that his mother, [[person:catherine-de-medici]], and his brother Anjou had had their share in the attempt. The Huguenots, she says, were threatening to take justice for themselves, and the king gave his consent to what followed only with the greatest difficulty, persuaded that his life and his kingdom depended on it [[cite:marguerite-4]] [[cite:marguerite-5]].

The historian De Thou, who was in Paris as a young man, writes that it was decided to "exterminate all the Protestants", that the queen mother approved, and that Navarre was to be spared. Guise told the captains it was the king's will. Armed men broke into Coligny's lodging, Besme, a retainer of the house of Guise, ran him through, and the body was thrown from the window, mutilated, dragged to the river and hung on the gibbet at Montfaucon [[cite:de-thou]]. Marguerite tells how a wounded man fled into her bedchamber with archers at his heels, and how another was killed at her feet [[cite:marguerite-5]].

Reports sent to England in September said that the killing in Paris lasted more than eight days and that "the opinion is" that above 3,000 died there, besides 400 gentlemen. They tell of more killing at Orléans, Lyon, Meaux and Rouen, and name the queen mother, Anjou, the duke of Nevers and Tavannes as those who devised it [[cite:csp-583]] [[cite:csp-584]]. The French authorities gave other accounts. A proclamation in Normandy on 28 August put the slaughter in Paris down to the private quarrel between Guise and the admiral [[cite:csp-545]]. In October the Parlement of Paris declared the dead Coligny guilty of treason and of a conspiracy against the king, and ordered a yearly thanksgiving in Paris on St Bartholomew's Day [[cite:csp-622]].

The same English reports say that the prince of Condé was to hear mass and that Navarre was being pressed to follow, and that Protestants in the south, no longer safe in their houses, had gathered at Nîmes and Montauban and were holding Catholics as hostages [[cite:csp-567]].
