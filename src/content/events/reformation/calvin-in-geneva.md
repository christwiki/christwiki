[[person:john-calvin]] left France, his native country, and was living quietly at [[place:basel]] when, as he later wrote, many were burned in France for their faith and pamphlets put it about that the victims were Anabaptists and rebels. He says that he published the *Institutes* to clear them, and that the book was then only a small treatise summarizing the chief truths of the Christian religion [[cite:memoir]]. He addressed it to King Francis I. His first purpose, he tells the king, had been to give his countrymen the rudiments of godliness; he now offers the book as a confession from which the king may learn what the doctrine is that is being punished with prison, exile and fire [[cite:address]].

By 1557, he says, it had grown into a much larger work [[cite:memoir]]. In its final form it opens: "Our wisdom, in so far as it ought to be deemed true and solid Wisdom, consists almost entirely of two parts: the knowledge of God and of ourselves" [[cite:knowledge]]. Its third book defines predestination as "the eternal decree of God, by which he determined with himself whatever he wished to happen with regard to every man", and adds that "some are preordained to eternal life, others to eternal damnation" [[cite:predestination]].

By his own account Calvin meant to spend a single night in [[place:geneva]] on the way to Strasbourg. [[person:guillaume-farel]], who with Pierre Viret had lately driven "Popery" from the city, pressed him to stay, and when entreaty failed declared that God would curse his retirement and his studies if he refused to help. Calvin says that he was struck with terror and stayed [[cite:memoir]]. The council register records that the two preachers would not accept the ceremonies that Bern pressed on the city and the councils adopted, that they preached after being forbidden, and that on 23 April 1538 the General Council voted that they leave the city within three days [[cite:banished]]. Calvin says that Martin Bucer then drew him back into the ministry [[cite:memoir]].

On 13 September 1541 the register notes that Calvin had arrived from Strasbourg, that he asked that the church be set in order and the order put in writing, and that the council resolved to ask him to stay for good [[cite:return]]. The resulting ordinances were passed by the General Council on 20 November [[cite:adopted]]. They name four offices which, they say, the Lord instituted for the government of his church: pastors, teachers, elders and deacons [[cite:orders]]. Twelve elders, chosen from the city's councils, are to watch over the life of everyone and to meet the ministers each Thursday. The councils added that the ministers have no civil jurisdiction and wield only the spiritual sword of the word of God [[cite:elders]].

On 27 October 1553 the council, having taken the advice of the churches of Bern, Basel, Zurich and Schaffhausen, condemned Michael Servetus, whom the ministers had had imprisoned for his heresies, to be burned alive at Champel, and the sentence was carried out [[cite:servetus]]. Later events in the Reformed churches include [[event:heidelberg-catechism]] and [[event:scottish-reformation]].
