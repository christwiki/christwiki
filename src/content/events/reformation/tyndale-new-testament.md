John Foxe tells that [[person:william-tyndale]], disputing with a learned man who set the pope's laws above God's, answered that if God spared his life he would cause "a boy that driveth the plough" to know more of Scripture than his opponent did [[cite:plough]]. Refused a place in the household of Cuthbert Tunstall, bishop of [[place:london]], he concluded, Foxe continues, that there was no place to translate the New Testament "in all England", and left for Germany [[cite:tunstall]].

A first printing was begun at Cologne. Its surviving sheets open with a prologue: "I have here translated, brethren and sisters, most dear and tenderly beloved in Christ, the New Testament, for your spiritual edifying, consolation, and solace" [[cite:prologue]]. Johannes Cochlaeus, an opponent of Luther then living in Cologne, wrote years later that he learned of the work from the printers, that the city's senate stopped it at his prompting, and that the two Englishmen fled up the Rhine to [[place:worms]] with the printed sheets [[cite:cochlaeus]].

The complete New Testament came out as a small book naming neither place nor printer; the library at Stuttgart, which holds the one copy with its title page, assigns it to the press of Peter Schöffer at Worms in 1526 [[cite:nt]]. On 11 August 1526 Georg Spalatin noted a report that six thousand copies of the New Testament in English had been printed at Worms [[cite:spalatin]]. In an address to the reader at the end, Tyndale says that he "had no man to counterfeit", no English version to follow, and asks that his work be counted "as a thing begun rather than finished" [[cite:reader]].

In a prohibition dated 23 October 1526, Tunstall ordered every copy in his diocese to be handed in within thirty days on pain of excommunication. Supporters of Luther's sect, he wrote, had mixed heretical articles and erroneous opinions into the translation [[cite:prohibition]]. [[person:thomas-more]] defended the burning of the book. It deserved to be called Tyndale's or Luther's testament, he argued, because it wrongly and with intent put "seniors" for priests, "congregation" for church and "love" for charity [[cite:more8]]. More added that the church's law did not forbid an English Bible as such, and that a translation by a sound Catholic scholar, approved by the bishops, would be good [[cite:more16]]. Tyndale's own argument, in a book of 1528, was that Scripture "ought to be in the mother tongue", as the law of Moses and the psalms had been in Israel's [[cite:obedience]].

Tyndale lived mostly at [[place:antwerp]] [[cite:foxe119]]. Foxe tells that he was betrayed, held for a year and a half in the castle of [[place:vilvoorde]], condemned under the emperor's decree, strangled at the stake and burned in 1536, crying, "Lord! open the king of England's eyes" [[cite:death]].

For the later English Bible see [[event:king-james-bible]].
