dating: |
  Jerome does not date his prefaces, and the years are worked out from his letters. He came to Rome with the bishops Paulinus and Epiphanius [[cite:arrival]], who had been summoned to a council there [[cite:council]]; the eastern bishops' reply to the same summons was written in 382 [[cite:summons]]. The preface to the Gospels is addressed to Damasus, who was dead when Jerome left the city [[cite:departure]]. Paula, with whom he settled at Bethlehem, died in January 404 after twenty years there [[cite:paula-death]], which puts the move in the middle of the 380s and the Gospel revision between 382 and then.

  For the Old Testament the fixed points are these. In the list of his works drawn up in the fourteenth year of Theodosius (392–393) Jerome already speaks of translating the Old Testament from the Hebrew [[cite:catalogue]]. A later letter, which the editors of the translation place in 398, says that everything is done except the first eight books [[cite:progress]]. The preface to Joshua reports the Pentateuch finished and was written after Paula's death [[cite:joshua]], which Jerome dates to 26 January in the sixth consulship of Honorius, AD 404 [[cite:paula-death]]. The work therefore ended in 404 or 405.

In the fourth century the Latin churches read the Old Testament in a version made from the Greek Septuagint [[cite:city]], and their copies of the Gospels differed widely from one another [[cite:gospels]]. [[person:jerome]], already known as a student of Scripture, came to [[place:rome]] with two eastern bishops and stayed to help its bishop, [[person:damasus-i]], with his correspondence [[cite:arrival]] [[cite:secretary]]. Damasus asked him to revise the Latin Gospels. In the preface Jerome says that there were "almost as many forms of texts as there are copies", that he compared old Greek manuscripts, and that he changed the familiar wording only where the sense was wrong. He expected to be called a forger [[cite:gospels]], and he was soon answering critics who charged him with correcting the Gospels against the authority of the ancients [[cite:critics]].

After Damasus died Jerome left Rome [[cite:departure]]. He settled at [[place:bethlehem]] with the Roman widow [[person:paula]], who founded monasteries there, and her daughter Eustochium; both women learned Hebrew [[cite:paula]]. He first revised Old Testament books from the Septuagint and then began to translate from the Hebrew, appealing to what he called the "Hebrew verity" [[cite:progress]]. The preface to Samuel and Kings, addressed to Paula and Eustochium, was meant as a "helmeted" introduction to the whole undertaking. It lists the twenty-two books of the Hebrew canon and says that Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and the Shepherd "are not in the canon" [[cite:helmeted]]. He translated Tobit and Judith all the same, from "Chaldean", that is Aramaic: Tobit in a single day at the request of two bishops, Judith in one night's work [[cite:tobit]] [[cite:judith]]. The books from Joshua to Ruth, with Esther, followed the Pentateuch and were issued after Paula's death [[cite:joshua]], which Jerome dates to January 404 [[cite:paula-death]].

Jerome described his usual method as rendering "sense for sense and not word for word", but made an exception for Scripture, "where even the order of the words is a mystery" [[cite:method]].

The new version was not welcomed everywhere. [[person:augustine]] wrote to Jerome that he would rather have a Latin translation of the Septuagint, the version the apostles used and the Greek churches read, and told him of a congregation at Oea in Africa that rose in protest when its bishop read Jonah in Jerome's wording [[cite:augustine]]. Years later Augustine praised Jerome as a man skilled in three languages and still held that no single translator should be preferred to the Seventy [[cite:city]].

Jerome's version nonetheless became the common text of the Latin church, and "vulgate" means the common edition. In 1546 the Council of Trent spoke of "the said old and vulgate edition" as approved by the usage of many years and declared it authentic for public reading, disputation and preaching [[cite:trent]].
