dating: |
  Patrick's two writings give no date, name no ruler whose dates are known and mention no datable event. He says only that he writes in old age [[cite:conf-old-age]] and that the Franks are still pagans [[cite:letter-franks]], which points to the fifth century.

  The years on the timeline come from the Irish annals, compiled centuries later. The Annals of Ulster put the sending of Palladius in 431 and Patrick's arrival in 432 [[cite:annals-432]]. They then enter his death three times: under 457 for "the elder Patrick", under 461 with the words "here some record", and under 492 and 493, where he is said to have died in the sixtieth year after he came [[cite:annals-death]]. The annalists record more than one tradition, and historians remain divided between a death about 461 and one about 493. J. B. Bury, *The Life of St. Patrick and His Place in History* (1905), argued for 461; the essays in David N. Dumville and others, *Saint Patrick, A.D. 493–1993* (1993), collect the evidence and favour 493. The annal for 432 itself appeals to the reckoning of later chroniclers, Bede, Marcellinus and Isidore [[cite:annals-432]].

Almost everything that can be known about [[person:patrick]] comes from two short Latin texts he wrote himself: a *Confession* composed in old age [[cite:conf-old-age]] and an open letter to the soldiers of a ruler called Coroticus [[cite:letter-bishop]].

He was a Briton: he writes of his parents and his home country in Britain [[cite:conf-call]] [[cite:conf-stay]]. He was the son of a deacon named Calpornius and grandson of a priest, from a village he calls Bannavem Taburniae; his father also held the civic rank of decurion [[cite:conf-home]] [[cite:letter-decurion]]. At about sixteen he was carried off to Ireland as a captive, with thousands of others [[cite:conf-home]]. For six years he herded animals, and he writes that in those years he began to pray, by day and by night. A voice in his sleep told him that a ship was ready; he walked some two hundred miles to find it and got away [[cite:conf-slave]].

Some years later, at home in Britain, he saw in a dream a man called Victoricus carrying letters from Ireland. As he read one he seemed to hear the people who lived beside the wood of Voclut, near the western sea, calling him to come and walk among them again [[cite:conf-call]]. He went back as a bishop [[cite:letter-bishop]]. He writes that he baptised thousands, ordained clergy, travelled to places where no one had yet come to baptise, gave gifts to kings and judges, and was once robbed and held in irons for fourteen days [[cite:conf-work]]. Sons and daughters of Irish rulers became monks and virgins of Christ, and so did women held in slavery, who suffered most for it [[cite:conf-converts]].

His mission had critics. At a hearing held in his absence his seniors brought up a sin of his boyhood, confessed before he was made deacon and disclosed by a close friend [[cite:conf-trial]]. The *Confession* is his answer. He wished to visit Britain and Gaul but believed himself bound to stay in Ireland for life [[cite:conf-stay]], and he calls the work a gift of God [[cite:conf-end]]. When the soldiers of Coroticus killed some of his newly baptised converts and carried off others, he told Christians not to eat or drink with them or take their alms until they did penance and freed the captives [[cite:letter-bishop]] [[cite:letter-ban]].

Patrick was not the only bishop sent to Ireland. Bede and the Irish annals both record that Pope Celestine sent Palladius as first bishop to the Irish, and Bede's notice speaks of Irish who already believed in Christ [[cite:palladius]] [[cite:annals-432]]. Nor does Patrick name any church he founded. The link with [[place:armagh]] is found some two centuries later, in the Life by Muirchú, where a local lord gives him the hill on which the church stands [[cite:muirchu-armagh]]. In the sixth century Columba, a monk from Ireland, crossed to Britain to preach to the northern Picts [[cite:bede-columba]] ([[event:columba-on-iona]]).
