dating: |
  The span runs from Basil's election as bishop of Caesarea to the council of Constantinople. Gregory of Nyssa says that Basil died eight years after he became bishop, and that a synod at Antioch met about nine months after his death [[cite:macrina-death]]. W. K. Lowther Clarke, in the introduction to his translation of the Life (1916), places that synod in September 379 and Basil's death on 1 January 379, which gives about 370 for the election. The law of Theodosius that names Gregory of Nyssa is dated 30 July 381 [[cite:law]]. Basil's *On the Holy Spirit* and the five Theological Orations of Gregory of Nazianzus carry no dates; the translators in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers connect the first with a request made by Amphilochius of Iconium in 374 and put the second between 379 and 381, at Constantinople.

[[person:basil-of-caesarea]] and his brother [[person:gregory-of-nyssa]] came from a large family with property in three provinces [[cite:macrina-basil]]. Gregory writes that when Basil came home from his studies, proud of his rhetoric, their elder sister [[person:macrina]] won him to the ascetic life, and that she then made the family household a community in which mistress and servants lived alike [[cite:macrina-basil]]. Basil had studied at Athens with his friend [[person:gregory-of-nazianzus]] [[cite:athens]]. He travelled to see the monks of Egypt, Palestine, Syria and Mesopotamia [[cite:monks]], founded monasteries in his own country [[cite:athens]], and as bishop of [[place:caesarea-in-cappadocia]] built a hospice for travellers and the sick, with physicians and attendants [[cite:hospice]]. Gregory of Nazianzus called it "the new city" [[cite:new-city]].

The emperor Valens held a creed opposed to that of Nicaea and had Basil brought before his prefect [[cite:athens]]. Gregory of Nazianzus describes Basil before the prefect, answering threats of confiscation, exile and death by saying that none of them could touch a man who owned nothing [[cite:prefect]].

They sought words for what Nicaea had confessed. Basil distinguished *ousia*, essence, from *hypostasis* as the general from the particular: one essence in God, and three hypostases, Father, Son and Holy Spirit [[cite:hypostasis]]. When he was attacked for praising the Father "with the Son together with the Holy Ghost" [[cite:doxology]], he wrote *On the Holy Spirit* to show that the Spirit shares the glory of Father and Son and that this does not make a second God [[cite:monarchy]]. Gregory of Nazianzus says that Basil, under the pressure of the times, avoided calling the Spirit God in so many words and left that to him [[cite:economy]]. Gregory did so: "Is the Spirit God? Most certainly" [[cite:spirit]]. He explained that the Old Testament proclaimed the Father openly and the Son more obscurely, that the New revealed the Son and hinted at the deity of the Spirit, and that the Spirit now gives a clearer proof of himself [[cite:stages]]. Gregory of Nyssa answered the charge of teaching three gods: every act of God comes from the Father through the Son in the Spirit, and the three are distinguished only as cause and caused [[cite:not-three]].

Basil died before the outcome [[cite:macrina-death]]. In 381 a council met at [[place:constantinople]], where Gregory of Nazianzus was briefly bishop before resigning [[cite:resignation]], and a law of Theodosius named Gregory of Nyssa among the bishops whose communion marked the true faith [[cite:law]]. The creed linked with that council confesses the Spirit as one "who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified" [[cite:creed]], and the bishops' letter of the next year speaks of one Godhead, power and substance of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit in "three perfect persons" [[cite:synod-382]]. See [[event:council-of-constantinople-i]].

Basil's rules for monastic communities were also read in the West: the Rule of Benedict commends "the rule of our holy Father, Basil" [[cite:benedict]].
