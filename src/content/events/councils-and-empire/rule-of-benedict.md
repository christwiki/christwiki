dating: |
  The Rule carries no date and names no person or place. Gregory the Great says that Benedict wrote a rule for his monks [[cite:dial-rule]] and tells of it after the move to Monte Cassino [[cite:dial-cassino]], but he gives no years either. A fixed point is the visit of King Totila, which Gregory tells among the events at Cassino [[cite:dial-totila]]: the continuation of Marcellinus, paraphrased here from the Latin, enters Totila's accession under 542 [[cite:totila-king]], so Benedict was alive and an abbot in the 540s. The years often given for the founding of Monte Cassino (about 529) and for Benedict's death (about 547) are not in Gregory and rest on later tradition. The Rule is therefore placed between about 530 and 550.

Almost all that is known of [[person:benedict-of-nursia]] comes from the second book of the *Dialogues* of Pope Gregory the Great, written about 593 from the reports of four of Benedict's disciples. Gregory says that he was born in the district of Nursia, was sent to Rome to study, and left his books to live alone in a cave at Subiaco [[cite:dial-youth]]. Disciples gathered, and he set up twelve small monasteries there, each with twelve monks [[cite:dial-subiaco]].

Driven out by the envy of a local priest, Benedict moved with a few monks to the hill above Cassino. There he broke the idol of Apollo, to whom the country people still sacrificed, and built oratories of Saint Martin and Saint John in its place [[cite:dial-cassino]]. This became the monastery of [[place:monte-cassino]]. The Gothic king Totila came to see him there [[cite:dial-totila]]. His sister [[person:scholastica]], dedicated to God from childhood, visited him once a year; Gregory tells how at their last meeting her prayer brought a storm that kept him talking with her through the night, and how three days later he saw her soul rise to heaven like a dove [[cite:dial-scholastica]]. Benedict died in the oratory, held upright by his monks at prayer, and was buried beside her [[cite:dial-scholastica]] [[cite:dial-death]].

Gregory adds that Benedict wrote a rule for monks, "excellent for discretion" [[cite:dial-rule]]. The Rule calls the monastery "a school of the Lord's service" in which the author hopes to lay down "nothing harsh or burdensome" [[cite:prologue]]. It is written for cenobites, monks who live together under a rule and an abbot [[cite:rule-1]]. The abbot holds the place of Christ and will answer to God for his monks, and he is to call the whole community to counsel on weighty matters [[cite:rule-2]]. A newcomer promises stability, conversion of morals and obedience [[cite:rule-58]], and no monk may own anything [[cite:rule-33]]. The day turns on the "Work of God", seven offices by day and one at night [[cite:rule-16]], with fixed hours for manual labour and for reading, because "idleness is the enemy of the soul" [[cite:rule-48]]. A long chapter sets out twelve degrees of humility [[cite:rule-7]], and guests are to be received as Christ [[cite:rule-53]].

The Rule ends by calling itself a beginning and sends the monk on to the teachers before it, among them "our holy Father, Basil" [[cite:rule-73]] ([[event:cappadocian-fathers]]). Its later spread through the West is told under [[event:carolingian-renewal]] and [[event:cluny]].
