  Socrates says the bishops assembled in May, in the consulship of Eucherius and Evagrius, which is 381 [[cite:socrates]]. The heading of the canons in the Greek collections dates them to the seventh day before the Ides of July in the same consulship, 9 July 381 [[cite:canon1]], and the emperor's law naming the bishops with whom the churches were to be in communion is dated 30 July [[cite:law]]. After taking the churches of [[place:constantinople]] from the opponents of Nicaea, [[person:theodosius-i]] summoned the bishops of his own, eastern, part of the empire to confirm the Nicene faith and to give the capital a bishop [[cite:socrates]] [[cite:summons]]. About 150 came. Thirty-six bishops of the Macedonian party, who would not call the Son of one substance with the Father, were invited as well; they refused the Nicene creed and went home [[cite:socrates]].

The council met in May 381. [[person:gregory-of-nazianzus]], who had gathered the Nicene congregation in the city, was confirmed as its bishop, and Meletius of Antioch died while the council sat [[cite:theodoret]] [[cite:meletius]]. Sozomen says that Gregory gave up the see when the Egyptian bishops and others objected [[cite:resignation]]; Socrates has him resign before the council met [[cite:earlier]]. His farewell address to the 150 bishops survives [[cite:farewell]]. In Sozomen's account the emperor then chose Nectarius, a senator not yet baptised, from a list drawn up by the bishops [[cite:nectarius]]; in Socrates's the people seized on him [[cite:socrates]].

The bishops reported to Theodosius that they had ratified the faith of Nicaea, condemned the heresies opposed to it and drawn up canons, and they asked him to confirm their work [[cite:letter]]. The first canon declares that the faith of the 318 fathers of Nicaea shall stand and anathematises, among others, the Arians, the Pneumatomachi or "fighters against the Spirit", and the Apollinarians [[cite:canon1]]. The second forbids bishops to intervene in churches outside their own region [[cite:canon2]]. The third gives the bishop of Constantinople "the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome; because Constantinople is New Rome" [[cite:canon3]].

The canons and the letter contain no creed, and the council's minutes are lost. The creed that carries its name is known from the Council of Chalcedon in 451, which set "the Creed of the one hundred and fifty holy Fathers who were assembled at Constantinople" beside that of Nicaea [[cite:chalcedon]]. It is not the text of 325. That creed said only "And in the Holy Ghost" and ended with anathemas [[cite:nicaea]]. This one has no anathemas and confesses the Spirit as "the Lord and Giver-of-Life, who proceedeth from the Father, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified" [[cite:creed]]. The bishops who met again in 382 summed up the same faith for [[person:damasus-i|Damasus of Rome]] and other western bishops as one Godhead, power and substance of Father, Son and Holy Spirit in three perfect hypostases [[cite:letter382]].

Under the name of the Nicene Creed this text became the common confession; the Catholic catechism of 1992 says that it remains common to all the great churches of East and West [[cite:catechism]]. One clause is not common to all. In the West the words "and the Son" were added to the statement that the Spirit proceeds from the Father; they are absent from the Greek text [[cite:western]].
