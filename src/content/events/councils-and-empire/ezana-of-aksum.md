dating: |
  The sources cited here give no date for Ezana's conversion. Two fixed points frame it. Rufinus says that when Frumentius came to Alexandria to ask for a bishop, Athanasius had "recently" entered on his office [[cite:rufinus-ordination]]; Athanasius became bishop in 328 [[cite:index]]. The letter of the emperor Constantius to Aezanes and Sazanes, which treats Frumentius as bishop of Aksum already, is quoted in a work Athanasius finished in 356–357 [[cite:constantius-letter]]. The conversion therefore falls between about 330 and 356.

  The two witnesses are hard to reconcile, since the letter reads as if the ordination were recent. Some scholars think Rufinus placed it too early; others take his "recently" to mean one of Athanasius's returns from exile, in 338 or 346. Ezana's inscriptions and coins show both pre-Christian and Christian forms: some coin issues carry the disc and crescent, others the cross. See Aaron Michael Butts, "Ezana of Aksum: The First Christian African King", *Aethiopica* 28 (2025), pp. 7–38, who sets out the evidence and the earlier literature and argues for a more cautious reading of Rufinus than has been usual.

The evidence from [[place:aksum]] itself is the king's own. In a long inscription cut in Greek and, in two different scripts, in Ge'ez, [[person:ezana]] calls himself king of the Aksumites, the Himyarites and other peoples, "king of kings" and son of the unconquered god Ares, and ends by dedicating statues to Ares in thanks for a victory [[cite:pagan-inscription]]. A second Greek inscription of the same king records a campaign against the Noba [[cite:noba-campaign]]. It begins differently: with the faith of God and the power of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and with Jesus Christ as the king's helper [[cite:christian-inscription]]. The online record of the stone does not transcribe these opening lines; they are given here from the translation quoted by Aaron Butts. The same king thus appears both as son of Ares and as a Christian.

The story of how the faith reached his court comes from outside. Rufinus, writing in Italy about 402, says he had it from Aedesius, one of the two men concerned, who was by then a priest at Tyre. A philosopher of Tyre sailed to "India" with two boys he was educating, Aedesius and [[person:frumentius]]. On the way home the ship put in at a port and the crew were killed. The boys were spared and taken to the king, who made Aedesius his cupbearer and put Frumentius in charge of his accounts and records. When the king died, the queen asked them to help govern until her son grew up. Frumentius sought out Christians among the Roman merchants and encouraged them to build places of prayer [[cite:rufinus]].

When the prince came of age the two left. Frumentius went to [[place:alexandria]] and urged the bishop to send a bishop to the Christians there. [[person:athanasius]] ordained Frumentius himself and sent him back, and, Rufinus says, a great number were converted [[cite:rufinus-ordination]]. Rufinus names neither the kingdom nor its king. Socrates repeats his account a generation later [[cite:socrates]].

Athanasius supplies the names. Writing in 356–357, while in hiding, he reports that the emperor Constantius II had written to the rulers of Aksum demanding that Frumentius, "Bishop of Auxumis", be sent to Egypt [[cite:apology-report]]. He quotes the letter. It is addressed to Aezanes and Sazanes, says that Frumentius was ordained by Athanasius, and requires him to be sent to Egypt and examined by Bishop George [[cite:constantius-letter]]. The inscription to Ares names among the king's brothers a Saiazana, a name close to the Sazanes of the letter [[cite:pagan-inscription]].

The church of Aksum thus began with a bishop ordained at Alexandria, and within a generation of [[person:constantine]] a Roman emperor was writing to its rulers about the doctrine to be taught in their churches [[cite:constantius-letter]].
