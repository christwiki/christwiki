  The day comes from the *Chronicon Paschale*, a Greek chronicle compiled in the seventh century, three hundred years after the event. Under the consuls Gallicanus and Symmachus, which is 330, it says that Constantine named the city on the fifth day before the Ides of May, a Monday, and ordered its birthday to be kept every year on that day, the eleventh of the month [[cite:paschale]]. 11 May 330 was in fact a Monday, which supports the entry. Jerome's Chronicle, written about fifty years after the event, puts the dedication in the same year [[cite:jerome]].

[[person:constantine]] besieged his rival Licinius in Byzantium, the old Greek city on the European shore of the Bosphorus, and defeated him across the strait at Chrysopolis [[cite:origo-war]]. An anonymous Latin account of his reign says that he renamed Byzantium after himself in memory of that victory, adorned it as if it were his native city, wished to make it equal to Rome, brought in new citizens from every quarter and gave it a senate [[cite:origo-city]].

Socrates, writing in the city a century later, says that Constantine enlarged it, surrounded it with great walls and decreed by a law, inscribed on a pillar in a public square, that it should be called New Rome [[cite:socrates]]. The *Chronicon Paschale* dates the ceremony. On 11 May 330 the emperor named the city [[place:constantinople]], held chariot races at which he wore a diadem of pearls and precious stones, and ordered the day to be kept each year as the city's birthday. On that day a gilded statue of Constantine holding the Fortune of the city was to be drawn into the hippodrome with an escort of soldiers carrying candles, and the reigning emperor was to rise and do it reverence [[cite:paschale]].

The writers describe the religion of the new city in different ways. Eusebius says that Constantine filled it with churches and shrines of the martyrs and allowed no images to be worshipped in temples, no altars and no sacrifices. He mentions fountains with figures of the Good Shepherd and of Daniel, and a jewelled cross set in the ceiling of the palace [[cite:eusebius]]. Socrates names two churches the emperor built, Irene and the Apostles, and adds that he brought the statues of the gods out of their temples to decorate the streets and set up the tripods of Delphi in the hippodrome [[cite:socrates]]. Jerome notes that the city was dedicated "by denuding nearly every other city" [[cite:jerome]].

Constantine was buried in Constantinople [[cite:origo-burial]]. The city's standing in the state became standing in the church: the council that met there in 381 ruled that its bishop should have "the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome; because Constantinople is New Rome" [[cite:canon3]].
