In 311 the emperor Galerius had ended the persecution with an edict that allowed Christians to exist again and to rebuild their meeting places [[cite:lact-galerius]]. After his victory at the Milvian Bridge, [[person:constantine]] went to [[place:milan]] that winter. [[person:licinius]], who ruled in Illyricum, came there to marry Constantine's sister Constantia [[cite:lact-milan]] [[cite:origo-wedding]].

While the wedding was being celebrated, Maximinus Daia, who ruled the lands beyond the Bosphorus, marched out of Syria and invaded Licinius's territory [[cite:lact-milan]]. Licinius marched to meet him and defeated him on the last day of April 313 [[cite:lact-battle]]. He then crossed into Bithynia, entered [[place:nicomedia]], and on 13 June ordered a letter addressed to the governor to be posted there [[cite:lact-letter]].

The letter speaks in the names of both emperors. It says that when they met at Milan they resolved to grant to Christians and to everyone else the freedom to follow whatever religion each person chose, so that whatever divinity there is in heaven might be favourable to them and to their subjects. Earlier restrictions on Christians are cancelled. Places of assembly that had been confiscated are to be handed back without payment, whether they had since been bought or received as gifts, and the present holders may apply for compensation from the emperors' bounty. Other property that belonged to the churches as corporate bodies is to be returned in the same way [[cite:lact-letter]].

This is the text commonly called the Edict of Milan. In form it is a letter to a provincial governor, and the copy that Lactantius gives, in Latin, is the one Licinius had posted at Nicomedia [[cite:lact-letter]]. Eusebius gives the letter in a Greek translation, with an opening passage that Lactantius does not have [[cite:eus-letter]]. Neither writer gives a law issued at Milan itself. Eusebius also preserves a letter of Constantine to the proconsul of Africa ordering that property of the churches be restored [[cite:eus-anulinus]].

The letter went beyond what Galerius had allowed: it granted the same freedom of worship to Christians and to all others, and gave the churches back what they had lost [[cite:lact-galerius]] [[cite:lact-letter]]. It did not make Christianity the religion of the empire. Licinius later turned against the Christians in his own territory, according to Eusebius [[cite:eus-licinius]], and Constantine defeated him and became sole emperor [[cite:origo-war]].
