  Eusebius, who was present, says that the church was dedicated in the thirtieth year of Constantine's reign and that the bishops came to Jerusalem straight from a council at Tyre [[cite:vc-dedication]]. Constantine was proclaimed emperor on his father's death on 25 July 306 [[cite:accession]], so his thirtieth year began in July 335, and the ancient index to the letters of Athanasius has him set out for the council of Tyre on 11 July and reach Constantinople on 30 October, in the months before the Easter of 336 [[cite:tyre]]. The dedication therefore fell in the late summer or autumn of 335. The translators of Egeria note that an old Armenian lectionary places the feast of the dedication on 23 September [[cite:egeria]]; Eusebius does not give the day.

Eusebius, the bishop of nearby Caesarea, tells the story as a contemporary. The place of the resurrection in [[place:jerusalem]], he says, had long before been buried under a mound of earth, paved over and crowned with a shrine of Venus. [[person:constantine]] ordered the shrine pulled down and the soil carted away, and when the old ground level was reached a cave tomb appeared, "contrary to all expectation" [[cite:vc-site]]. The emperor wrote to the bishop, [[person:macarius-of-jerusalem]], that a church finer than any other should be built there. The governors were to supply workmen, and Macarius was to report what columns and marble were needed and whether he wanted a panelled ceiling, which could be gilded [[cite:vc-letter]].

A pilgrim from Bordeaux who came in 333 saw the little hill of Golgotha, the tomb a stone's throw from it, and a basilica "of wondrous beauty" built there by Constantine's order [[cite:bordeaux]]. Eusebius describes the finished complex: the tomb itself adorned with columns, an open court with colonnades, and to the east a tall basilica with a gilded ceiling, colonnades on either side and a hemisphere ringed by twelve columns [[cite:vc-building]].

In 335 the emperor summoned the bishops who were meeting at Tyre to go on to Jerusalem for the dedication. Eusebius, who preached there, names bishops from Macedonia to Persia and sets the feast in the thirtieth year of Constantine's reign [[cite:vc-dedication]]. Socrates adds that the same gathering received Arius back into communion [[cite:socrates]].

Eusebius also reports that the emperor's mother, [[person:helena]], visited Palestine in old age and founded churches at Bethlehem and on the Mount of Olives [[cite:vc-helena]]. In these chapters he does not connect her with the tomb or mention a cross. About 350 Cyril, preaching in the new basilica, could say that pieces of the wood of the cross had filled the whole world [[cite:cyril]], and in the 380s the pilgrim Egeria was told that the church had been dedicated on the day the cross was found [[cite:egeria]]. Neither passage names Helena. The story that she found it is told at the end of the century. Ambrose, in 395, has her dig at Golgotha, uncover three crosses and know Christ's by its title [[cite:ambrose]]. Rufinus, a few years later, says that Bishop Macarius identified it when its touch healed a dying woman [[cite:rufinus]].

The buildings fixed the place where Christians have since kept the memory of the death and resurrection of Jesus. In Egeria's time the anniversary of the dedication was an eight-day feast that drew monks and lay people from Mesopotamia, Syria and Egypt [[cite:egeria]].
