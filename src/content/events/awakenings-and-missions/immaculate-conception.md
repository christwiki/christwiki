Whether Mary was conceived free of original sin was long disputed among Latin theologians. [[person:thomas-aquinas]] taught that she was sanctified in the womb, but only after her soul had been infused: had her soul never incurred the stain of original sin, "this would be derogatory to the dignity of Christ, by reason of His being the universal Saviour of all" [[cite:aquinas]]. John Duns Scotus argued from the same ground, the excellence of Christ as redeemer, to the opposite conclusion: the most perfect mediator would mediate most perfectly for his mother, and that means meriting to preserve her from original sin [[cite:scotus-mediator]]. God, he said, could have kept her from it altogether, or cleansed her after an instant or after a time; which he did God knows, but unless the authority of the church or of Scripture stood against it, it seemed probable to attribute to Mary what is more excellent [[cite:scotus-answer]].

In 1483 [[person:sixtus-iv]], noting that the Roman church kept the feast of Mary's conception, condemned as false the assertion of some preachers that those who hold her to have been conceived without original sin commit mortal sin, or that those who keep the office of the feast are heretics [[cite:sixtus]]. He also reproved those who charged the contrary opinion with heresy, because the Roman church and the apostolic see had not yet decided [[cite:sixtus]]. The Council of Trent, in its decree on original sin of 1546, declared that it did not intend to include "the blessed and immaculate Virgin Mary" and renewed the constitutions of Sixtus [[cite:trent]].

In February 1849 [[person:pius-ix]], writing from Gaeta, asked the bishops how their clergy and people regarded the doctrine and what they themselves thought about defining it [[cite:ubi-primum]]. He says that they answered "with one voice" in favour [[cite:bishops]]. In the letter *Ineffabilis Deus*, given at St Peter's in [[place:rome]] on 8 December 1854, he declared, pronounced and defined that the doctrine which holds that Mary, in the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege of almighty God and in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the saviour of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin, is revealed by God and therefore to be believed firmly and constantly by all the faithful [[cite:definition]].

The bishop of Tarbes wrote in 1862 that [[person:bernadette-soubirous]], a child of fourteen born at [[place:lourdes]], had by her own account, taken from her mouth before his commission, seen and heard a being that called itself the Immaculate Conception ("se disant l'Immaculée-Conception") [[cite:bernadette]]. He judged that Mary had really appeared to her eighteen times in a grotto near the town, beginning on 11 February 1858 [[cite:judgment]]. The sanctuary at Lourdes gives the words as Bernadette recounted them, *Que soy era Immaculada Concepciou*, and dates them to 25 March 1858 [[cite:words]].

Statements of other churches rejected or questioned the definition. The patriarch of Constantinople and his synod wrote in 1895 that the "Papal Church" had made "an innovation" by laying down a dogma that the ancient church did not know [[cite:orthodox]]. The Old Catholic bishops at Utrecht rejected it in 1889 as without foundation in Scripture and in the tradition of the first centuries [[cite:utrecht]]. A paper written for the Church of England's General Synod in 2011 says that many Anglicans and other Christians found the definitions of 1854 and 1950 without a proper basis in the New Testament and the early church, and denied the pope's authority to define the faith of the whole church [[cite:synod]]. The Anglican–Roman Catholic commission recorded in 1981 that the two dogmas, promulgated by the bishop of Rome apart from a synod, raise a special problem for Anglicans who do not consider their precise definitions sufficiently supported by Scripture [[cite:arcic]]; see [[event:first-vatican-council|the council of 1869–1870]] and [[event:dogma-of-the-assumption|the definition of 1950]].
