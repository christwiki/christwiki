[[person:barton-stone]] was the Presbyterian minister of two congregations in Bourbon County, Kentucky, Cane Ridge and Concord [[cite:settled]]. In the spring of 1801, hearing of a "religious excitement" in the south of the state under James McGready and other Presbyterian ministers, he went to a camp-meeting in Logan County, where he saw many fall "as men slain in battle" and lie for hours as if dead, then rise "shouting deliverance" and exhort the crowd [[cite:logan]]. He judged it "a good work—the work of God", though he adds that he saw then and later much that he thought fanaticism [[cite:logan]].

When he told his own people what he had seen, the same things began among them, and the effect, he says, was "like fire in dry stubble driven by a strong wind" [[cite:return]]. A great meeting was appointed at [[place:cane-ridge]] for August 1801 [[cite:meeting]]. Stone describes roads crowded with wagons, carriages, riders and people on foot; four or five preachers speaking at once in different parts of the camp; and Methodist and Baptist preachers working alongside the Presbyterians, all urging "free salvation" by faith and repentance; military men on the ground, he reports, judged the crowd at between twenty and thirty thousand [[cite:meeting]]. The meeting went on for six or seven days and nights and ended when food ran short [[cite:meeting]]. He lists the bodily "exercises" seen in those years: falling, the jerks, dancing, barking, laughing and singing [[cite:exercises]].

People who had come from Ohio and other distant parts carried the same spirit home with them [[cite:meeting]]. Stone and several colleagues then withdrew from the Synod of Kentucky, which suspended them, and he records "great commotion and division in the churches" [[cite:presbytery]]. They formed the Springfield Presbytery, rejecting "all authoritative creeds, but the Bible alone"; within about a year they gave up that name too and called themselves simply Christians [[cite:presbytery]]. Their *Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery* is dated 28 June 1804: "We will, that this body die, be dissolved, and sink into union with the Body of Christ at large" [[cite:last-will]].

A generation later [[person:charles-finney]], an evangelist who had become a pastor, gave a course of lectures to his congregation, which the editor of a religious newspaper took down [[cite:finney-preface]]; they were in print by 1835 [[cite:finney-title]]. A revival, he taught, "is not a miracle, or dependent on a miracle, in any sense. It is a purely philosophical result of the right use of the constituted means" [[cite:finney-revival]]. He defended new measures such as the "anxious seat", a place set apart where those troubled about their souls could come forward to be spoken with and prayed for [[cite:finney-measures]].

Stone wrote that he had always believed the work to be of God [[cite:logan]]; Finney taught his hearers to expect revivals wherever the appointed means were rightly used [[cite:finney-revival]]. For the earlier revivals see [[event:great-awakening]] and [[event:wesley-and-methodism]].
