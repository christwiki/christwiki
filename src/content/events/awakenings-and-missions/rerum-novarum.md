[[person:leo-xiii]] dated his encyclical on capital and labour at St Peter's in [[place:rome]] on 15 May 1891 [[cite:dated]]. It begins from the conflict of the age: the growth of industry, the changed relations of masters and workmen, the enormous fortunes of a few and the poverty of the masses [[cite:opening]]. A remedy, the pope says, must be found quickly for the misery pressing unjustly on the majority of the working class [[cite:opening]]. The old guilds had been abolished and nothing had replaced them, working men had been left isolated and helpless before the greed of unchecked competition, and a small number of very rich men had laid on the labouring poor "a yoke little better than that of slavery itself" [[cite:opening]].

The letter rejects the socialist remedy: to make all possessions the common property of the community, it says, would hurt the working man first and would rob lawful owners [[cite:socialism]]. Private ownership is in accordance with the law of nature, and the law should favour ownership and lead as many people as possible to become owners [[cite:property]]. It rejects equally the idea that class is naturally hostile to class: capital cannot do without labour, nor labour without capital [[cite:classes]].

Each side has duties. Workers are to do the work they have freely agreed to and never to use violence [[cite:duties]]. Employers are not to treat their workers as bondsmen, are to respect their dignity as persons, and must not tax them beyond their strength; to defraud anyone of wages that are due is a crime that cries to heaven [[cite:duties]]. Public authority must step in when the general interest or any class is harmed and the harm cannot otherwise be met, and the poor have a claim to its special care [[cite:state]]. Hours of work must not exceed what strength allows, children must not be put into workshops before they are sufficiently grown, and Sunday is to be a day of rest [[cite:hours]]. Wages may be freely agreed, yet a dictate of natural justice older than any bargain requires that they be enough to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner [[cite:wage]]. The most important of the associations that can help are workingmen's unions, and to form such societies is a natural right, which the state exists to protect [[cite:unions]].

The year before, the Protestant [[person:william-booth]] had written that the usual remedies of Christian philanthropy were "lamentably inadequate" to the misery of the poor [[cite:booth]]; see [[event:salvation-army]]. Forty years later Pius XI called Leo's letter the "Magna Charta" on which Christian activity in the social field ought to be based [[cite:pius]].
