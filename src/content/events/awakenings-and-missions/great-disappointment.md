[[person:william-miller]] tells his own story in the *Apology and Defence* he published in 1845. For about twelve years he had defended the opinions of the Deists [[cite:deist]]. After serving as a captain in the United States army from 1813 until the peace, he settled at [[place:low-hampton]] in the state of New York [[cite:army]]. In 1816 he came to hold the Bible to be a revelation from God [[cite:converted]], and he gave about two years to reading it through with a concordance [[cite:study]]. He concluded that the 2300 days of Daniel's vision [[bible:Dan 8:13-14]] stood for years and began in 457 BC, so that they would end "about A. D. 1843" [[cite:reckoning]], and that in about twenty-five years the affairs of the present world would be wound up [[cite:conclusion]].

By his recollection he gave his first public lecture in August 1833, at Dresden, about sixteen miles from his home [[cite:first-lecture]]; his biographer says 1831 (see the dating note). In 1836 he wrote out sixteen lectures, which were printed at Troy [[cite:lectures-printed]] under the title *Evidence from Scripture and History of the Second Coming of Christ, about the Year 1843* [[cite:evidence-title]]; its third lecture does the sum: "take 457 from 2300 and it will leave A. D. 1843" [[cite:evidence-sum]]. Miller estimated in 1845 that about two hundred ministers had embraced his views and that nearly a thousand Advent congregations had been raised up, with some fifty thousand believers [[cite:numbers]]. He had never wished, he wrote, to set up an interest separate from the existing denominations [[cite:no-sect]].

He says that he was never positive about a day. In December 1842 he published his belief that the Lord would come between 21 March 1843 and 21 March 1844; that time passed, and "Our disappointment was great" [[cite:first-time]]. In 1844 came "the seventh month movement", as he says it was called, which looked to 22 October. Miller held back until two or three weeks before the day and then accepted it. "But that time passed; and I was again disappointed" [[cite:seventh-month]]. The following year he wrote that "we were certainly disappointed", that to contend otherwise was dishonest, and that he had no confidence in the new theories that had grown out of the movement, among them that Christ had then come as the Bridegroom or that the door of mercy was closed [[cite:mistaken]].

Some who had shared the expectation kept the date and gave it another meaning. [[person:ellen-white]], who had heard Miller lecture at Portland, Maine, when she was thirteen [[cite:white-youth]], relates that in December 1844, while she prayed with four other women, she was shown in a vision the Advent people travelling a narrow path to the holy city, lit from behind by a light that an angel told her was "the midnight cry" [[cite:white-vision]]. In a vision she dates to 24 March 1849 she saw, she says, that in 1844 Jesus had passed into the most holy place of the heavenly sanctuary, where the ark holds the ten commandments, and that God's people were now being tested on the Sabbath [[cite:white-sanctuary]].

In May 1863 delegates from six states met at [[place:battle-creek]], Michigan, and organized the General Conference of Seventh-day Adventists [[cite:conference]]. That church teaches today that in 1844, at the end of the prophetic period of 2300 days, Christ entered the second and last phase of his atoning ministry, a work of investigative judgment [[cite:belief-24]], and that the gift of prophecy was manifested in the ministry of Ellen G. White [[cite:belief-18]]. For earlier revivals see [[event:second-great-awakening|the Second Great Awakening]].
