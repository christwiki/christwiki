[[person:hudson-taylor]] first sailed for China in September 1853, sent by the Chinese Evangelisation Society, and landed at Shanghai on 1 March 1854 [[cite:early]]. He resigned from the society because it was often in debt, which he believed Scripture forbade, and went on working at Ningpo, depending, as he puts it, on God alone for supplies [[cite:resigned]].

Ill health forced him back to England, where, he writes, a map of China hung on his study wall, and he came to the conviction that a special agency was needed to evangelise the inland provinces, though for a long time he shrank from leading it [[cite:agency]]. On Sunday 25 June 1865, staying with a friend at [[place:brighton]], he left a congregation of a thousand or more and walked out alone on the sands [[cite:agency]]. He says that there he gave himself up for the work and asked for twenty-four fellow workers, two for each of eleven inland provinces that had no missionary and two for Mongolia, and that he wrote the request in the margin of his Bible [[cite:agency]].

That year he wrote *China's Spiritual Need and Claims*, and a later edition recalls that in the first he had asked for twenty-four European and twenty-four native evangelists [[cite:appeal]]. He writes that the China Inland Mission was organised in 1865, with a friend, W. T. Berger, directing it at home, and that he had resolved never to use personal solicitation or collections for its funds [[cite:formed]]. On 26 May 1866 a party of sixteen missionaries, with his four children, sailed in the *Lammermuir* [[cite:formed]].

The mission's history, quoting the letters of the party, records that they anchored off Shanghai on 30 September 1866 [[cite:shanghai]] and that on the journey inland all of them put on Chinese dress [[cite:dress]]. At the end of November, it says, they reached [[place:hangzhou]], where another missionary lent them a house [[cite:hangzhou]].

Writing in 1894, Taylor set out how the mission worked: candidates were accepted without restriction as to denomination and were promised no income, the mission would not go into debt, and its expenses were met by gifts sent without personal solicitation [[cite:principles]]. By May 1893, he says, it had 552 missionaries and had opened stations in ten of the eleven provinces that had been without Protestant missionaries [[cite:principles]].
