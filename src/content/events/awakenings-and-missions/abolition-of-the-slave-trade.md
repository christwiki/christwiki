In June 1783 the Quakers, gathered for their yearly meeting, petitioned the House of Commons against the slave trade; [[person:thomas-clarkson]] calls it the first petition for abolition ever presented to Parliament [[cite:quakers]]. [[person:john-wesley]] had written in 1774 that slave-holding could not be consistent with any degree of natural justice [[cite:wesley-justice]], and had called on captains, merchants and planters to give it up [[cite:wesley-appeal]].

Clarkson, a Cambridge graduate in deacon's orders, won a prize in 1785 for a Latin essay on whether it is right to enslave people against their will, and gave himself to the cause [[cite:essay]] [[cite:deacon]]. On 22 May 1787 a committee for abolishing the trade was formed in [[place:london]]; all but three of its members were Quakers, and [[person:william-wilberforce]], a member of Parliament, had agreed to raise the question in the Commons [[cite:committee]]. His sons print from his journal for 28 October 1787: "God Almighty has set before me two great objects, the suppression of the slave trade and the reformation of manners" [[cite:journal]].

In March 1789 [[person:olaudah-equiano]], who had been a slave, dedicated his autobiography to Parliament in the hope of relieving his "suffering countrymen" [[cite:dedication]]. He describes the hold of a slave ship and a sale at which brothers were parted, and asks the "nominal Christians" responsible whether they learned this from their God [[cite:crossing]].

Other Christians defended the trade. In 1788 Raymund Harris, by Clarkson's account a former clergyman and Jesuit [[cite:harris-clarkson]], dedicated to the corporation of Liverpool a pamphlet arguing that the trade agreed with the law of nature, the law of Moses and the Christian law [[cite:harris]]. A missionary society owned slaves. Bishop Beilby Porteus preached before the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in February 1783 [[cite:porteus-date]]. He spoke of "the slaves employed on their plantations" in Barbados, on lands left to the Society by General Codrington, and urged it to begin the work of conversion with them [[cite:porteus]].

Wilberforce brought the question before the Commons on 12 May 1789 [[cite:motion]]. In April 1791 his motion to stop the import of slaves from Africa was lost by 163 votes to 88 [[cite:defeat]]. On 23 February 1807 the Commons voted by 283 to 16 to proceed with a bill from the Lords, and it received the royal assent on 25 March [[cite:passed]]. The Act declared the African slave trade "utterly abolished, prohibited, and declared to be unlawful" from 1 May 1807 [[cite:act]]. It ended the British trade, not slavery: a further Act of 1833 declared the slaves in the British colonies free from 1 August 1834, bound them to work on as apprenticed labourers, and granted twenty million pounds to compensate their owners [[cite:act1833]]. In 1839 Pope Gregory XVI condemned the trade, wrote that numerous Christians still practised it, and forbade any Catholic to defend it as permissible [[cite:pope]].
