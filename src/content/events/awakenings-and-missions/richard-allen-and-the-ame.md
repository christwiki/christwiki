[[person:richard-allen]] tells his own story. He was born on 14 February 1760, a slave of Benjamin Chew of [[place:philadelphia]], was sold with his family into Delaware, was converted there and joined the Methodist society, and bought his freedom from his master [[cite:early]]. After some years as a travelling preacher he came to Philadelphia in February 1786, preached at St George's Church at five in the morning, and gathered a society of forty-two members among the Black people of the city [[cite:arrival]]. He proposed a separate place of worship for them, and three men joined him, among them [[person:absalom-jones]] [[cite:arrival]].

As Black worshippers at St George's grew in number they were moved from their seats to the wall and then sent to the gallery [[cite:walkout]]. One Sunday, Allen writes, they knelt there for prayer:

> We had not been long upon our knees before I heard considerable scuffling and low talking. I raised my head up and saw one of the trustees, H-- M--, having hold of the Rev. Absalom Jones, pulling him up off of his knees, and saying, "You must get up--you must not kneel here."

Jones asked to be left until the prayer was over, and a second trustee came to pull up another man. "By this time prayer was over, and we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church" [[cite:walkout]].

They hired a store-room, worshipped by themselves and raised money for a building. When the Methodist elder threatened to disown them, they answered that he could not "seal up the scriptures from us, and deny us a name in heaven" [[cite:store-room]]. When the members voted on which denomination to join, a large majority chose the Church of England; only Allen and Jones voted for the Methodists [[cite:vote]]. Bishop White ordained one of their number as pastor of that church [[cite:discipline-white]], and the title page of a sermon that Jones printed in 1808 names him rector of St Thomas's, the African Episcopal Church [[cite:jones-rector]]. Allen refused the post: he "could not be any thing else but a Methodist" [[cite:methodist]].

Allen bought an old blacksmith's shop, moved it to his own lot and fitted it for worship; Bishop Asbury opened it in July 1794, and it was named Bethel [[cite:bethel]]. For years the white Methodist elders of the city claimed control of its property and pulpit, until a suit before the Supreme Court ended in Bethel's favour [[cite:lawsuit]].

In April 1816 delegates from Baltimore and other places met those of Philadelphia and resolved to "become one body, under the name of the African Methodist Episcopal Church" [[cite:conference]]. On 11 April Allen was set apart as bishop by the laying on of hands of five ordained ministers [[cite:discipline-bishop]]. The new church's *Doctrines and Discipline*, printed in 1817, kept the Methodism that Allen said had first brought the gospel to his people [[cite:methodist]]; the church governed itself in order to be free of what its founders called "spiritual despotism" [[cite:discipline-union]], and refused membership to slaveholders [[cite:discipline-slavery]].
