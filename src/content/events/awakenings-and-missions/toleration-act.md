In [[place:london]] on 24 May 1689 [[person:william-iii]], seated on the throne before the Lords and the Commons, gave the royal assent to "An Act for exempting Their Majesties Protestant Subjects dissenting from the Church of England, from the Penalties of certain Laws" [[cite:assent]].

The Act says that some ease to scrupulous consciences in the exercise of religion may help to unite the king's and queen's Protestant subjects. It lists the laws of Elizabeth I and her successors that punished absence from the parish church and attendance at other religious meetings, and it provides that they shall not apply to dissenters who take the oaths appointed by an Act of the same Parliament and subscribe the declaration required by the Act of Charles II that kept Roman Catholics out of Parliament [[cite:terms]]. Their meetings must be held with the doors unlocked, in places certified to the bishop, the archdeacon or the justices, and they still owe tithes to the parish [[cite:meetings]]. Their ministers must subscribe the Articles of Religion, except the thirty-fourth, thirty-fifth and thirty-sixth and a clause of the twentieth on the church's power to decree rites and its authority in controversies of faith; those who scruple the baptism of infants are excused part of the twenty-seventh as well [[cite:ministers]]. Those who refuse all oaths, as the Quakers did, may make a declaration of fidelity and profess their faith in the Trinity and in Scripture instead [[cite:quakers]]. The Act gives no benefit to "any Papist or Popish Recusant whatsoever" or to anyone who denies the doctrine of the Trinity in his preaching or writing [[cite:excluded]].

In the same year [[person:john-locke]]'s *Letter Concerning Toleration* appeared in English [[cite:letter]]. Locke calls toleration "the chief characteristic mark of the true Church" [[cite:mark]]. The commonwealth, he argues, exists only to secure civil interests; the care of souls is not committed to the magistrate, because his power is outward force and true religion is the inward persuasion of the mind [[cite:magistrate]]. A church is "a voluntary society of men" [[cite:church]]. He sets limits of his own: a church whose members deliver themselves to the service of another prince has no right to toleration, and "those are not at all to be tolerated who deny the being of a God" [[cite:limits]]. Within those limits he writes that "neither Pagan nor Mahometan, nor Jew, ought to be excluded from the civil rights of the commonwealth because of his religion" [[cite:rights]].

The Act did not repeal the old laws. It lifted their penalties from those who met its conditions [[cite:terms]] and declared the laws on attending divine service on Sunday still in force against everyone who did not go to a congregation it allowed [[cite:inforce]]. In 1744 a pamphlet against the Methodists charged that their preachers had not qualified themselves under "the Act of Toleration" and were therefore defying the law [[cite:observations]]; see [[event:wesley-and-methodism]].
