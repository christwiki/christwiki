dating: |
  The inscription carries no date and its beginning is lost. If lines 7–9 are rightly restored, it looks back on the deaths of Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah, which fall in 841 BC, the year in which Joram's successor Jehu paid tribute to Assyria [[cite:year18]]. The stele was therefore set up after 841. Hazael was still king of Damascus three years later [[cite:year21]], and Kings has him oppress Israel through the reigns of Jehu and his son [[bible:2 Kings 10:32-33; 13:22]], so any year in the following decades is possible. The timeline places it about 840 BC.

  The excavator Avraham Biran and the epigrapher Joseph Naveh published the first fragment in "An Aramaic Stele Fragment from Tel Dan", *Israel Exploration Journal* 43 (1993), pp. 81–98, and the two found in June 1994 in "The Tel Dan Inscription: A New Fragment", *Israel Exploration Journal* 45 (1995), pp. 1–18. There they joined the pieces and restored the two royal names; the line numbers used here follow their arrangement. The stele has generally been assigned to Hazael. George Athas, *The Tel Dan Inscription: A Reappraisal and a New Interpretation* (2003), arranges the fragments differently, dates the stele about 796 BC, assigns it to Hazael's son Bar-hadad and takes *bytdwd* as a name for Jerusalem. Most scholars follow Biran and Naveh.

In July 1993 excavators at Tel Dan, the site of ancient [[place:dan]], found a broken piece of basalt with Aramaic writing built into a wall [[cite:find]]. Two smaller pieces turned up the following June; the excavators' reports are named in the note on dating. Together the pieces preserve parts of thirteen lines [[cite:lines]] of a royal victory stele, which is now in the Israel Museum [[cite:record]].

The speaker is a king whose name is lost. He says that the king of Israel had earlier entered his father's land, that his father died, that the god Hadad made him king and went before him, and that he killed kings who had thousands of chariots and horsemen [[cite:opening]]. Then come two names, both damaged. One ends in "-ram son of" and is followed, after a gap, by "king of Israel". The other ends in "-iahu son of" and is followed, after a gap, by "of the House of David" [[cite:kings]].

On the usual arrangement of the fragments, the king of Israel can only be Joram son of Ahab, the one king of Israel whose name ends in this way. The king of Judah who died with him was Ahaziah son of Jehoram, and the second name is restored accordingly. Kings says that the two were together at Ramoth-gilead fighting [[person:hazael]] of Damascus when Joram was wounded [[bible:2 Kings 8:28-29]], and that Hazael afterwards took Israel's land east of the Jordan [[bible:2 Kings 10:32-33]]. Hazael is therefore the likely author. One difficulty is that the speaker writes of "my father" as his predecessor, while an Assyrian inscription calls Hazael "son of a nobody" [[cite:nobody]] and Kings makes him a court official who took the throne [[bible:2 Kings 8:7-15]].

The stele and Kings disagree about the deaths. In Kings it is [[person:jehu]] who shoots Joram and orders Ahaziah struck down [[bible:2 Kings 9:24-27]]; on the stele, as it is usually restored, the Aramean king claims both [[cite:kings]]. The word "killed" survives before the second name; its subject, and the whole verb before the first name, are restored. Taken literally, the two accounts conflict. Kings itself links the two men, since [[person:elijah]] is told to anoint both Hazael and Jehu as agents of the same judgment [[bible:1 Kings 19:15-17]].

The words "House of David", written on the stone as a single word, *bytdwd*, are what made the find famous. On the usual reading, a neighbouring king, about a century and a half after [[person:david]], could name the kingdom of Judah after him, as Assyrian scribes named Israel after Omri. The inscription says nothing about David himself. It shows that his dynasty was known by his name, the "house" that Nathan's oracle promises to him [[bible:2 Sam 7:11-16]]. A similar reading has been proposed for a broken line of the Mesha Stele [[cite:mesha]], where it is far less certain; see [[event:mesha-stele]].
