dating: |
  Both Lives were written in Slavonic. They are paraphrased here from nineteenth-century Latin translations, that of F. Miklosich for the Life of Constantine (1870) and the one printed by J. A. Ginzel for the Life of Methodius (1861), which are the pages linked.

  Neither Life dates the arrival in Moravia. The fixed points are at the end: Constantine died at Rome on 14 February in the second indiction, the year 6377 from creation, that is AD 869 [[cite:death-of-cyril]], and Methodius on 6 April in the third indiction [[cite:death-of-methodius]]. The Latin translation linked here prints the year from creation as the 1393rd, a slip for the 6393 of the Slavonic text, that is AD 885; the third indiction ran from September 884. The brothers were received at Rome by Hadrian II [[cite:rome]], who succeeded Nicholas I at the end of 867.

  Counting back, the Life of Constantine gives forty months in Moravia [[cite:moravia]] and the Life of Methodius three years [[cite:thessalonians]], followed by a stay in Pannonia and the journey through Venice. That puts the arrival in 863 or 864; 863 is the year usually given. The papal letters are dated by month and indiction [[cite:industriae]].

In the early 860s [[person:rastislav]], ruler of the Moravians, sent to the emperor Michael III at Constantinople. His people, he said, had given up paganism and kept the Christian law, but had no teacher to explain the faith in their own language [[cite:embassy]]. The emperor chose Constantine, called the Philosopher, and his brother [[person:methodius]], natives of [[place:thessalonica]], where, the Life of Methodius has him say, everyone spoke good Slavonic [[cite:thessalonians]]. Constantine, known by his later monastic name as [[person:cyril]], devised letters for the language, and the Life says that the first words he wrote in them were the opening of John's Gospel [[cite:embassy]].

In [[place:great-moravia]] the brothers trained pupils and translated the services of the church. Latin clergy objected that God might be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Constantine called them followers of Pilate, who had written the title on the cross in those languages [[cite:moravia]]; the Life calls their view the heresy of the three tongues [[cite:venice]]. At Venice he asked his critics whether the rain does not fall on all alike, and quoted Paul on the uselessness of prayer that the hearers cannot understand [[cite:venice]] [[bible:1 Cor 14:14-19]].

They went on to [[place:rome]], bringing relics believed to be those of Clement of Rome. Pope [[person:hadrian-ii]] came out to meet them, laid the Slavonic books in the church of St Mary and had their pupils ordained, and the liturgy was sung in Slavonic in St Peter's [[cite:rome]]. Constantine fell ill there, became a monk under the name Cyril and died in February 869 [[cite:death-of-cyril]].

Methodius was sent back to the Slavs and, at the request of the prince Kocel, made bishop for Pannonia [[cite:hadrian]]. The Life of Methodius gives a letter of Hadrian allowing the liturgy in Slavonic provided that the Epistle and Gospel were first read in Latin; no Latin text of it survives, and the editor of the translation cited here marks it as spurious [[cite:hadrian]]. The Frankish king's bishops accused him of teaching in their territory and held him in Swabia for two and a half years, until the pope forced his release [[cite:trial]]. Pope John VIII's own letters show Rome's hesitation. In 879 he summoned Methodius and reminded him that he had already been forbidden to sing mass in Slavonic, which the letter calls a barbarous tongue, though he might preach in it [[cite:summons]]. In 880, having examined him, he declared him orthodox, praised the letters devised by Constantine and permitted mass and the offices in Slavonic, with the Gospel read first in Latin [[cite:industriae]].

Methodius finished translating the Scriptures [[cite:translation]] and died in 885 [[cite:death-of-methodius]]. A letter in the name of Stephen V then forbade the Slavonic liturgy under anathema [[cite:stephen]]. The work outlived the ban elsewhere: the chronicle of Kyiv, more than two centuries later, says that the books first written for the Moravians were those in use in Rus' and among the Bulgarians [[cite:chronicle]].
