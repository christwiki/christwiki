In the late 850s the patriarch [[person:ignatius-of-constantinople]] lost his see, and [[person:photius]], a layman in the imperial service, was made patriarch of [[place:constantinople]] in his place. How Ignatius came to leave office was disputed from the start. The account preserved in the pope's letters is that of Ignatius's supporters: Photius was tonsured and consecrated in haste, by a bishop already under censure, while the lawful patriarch was still alive [[cite:sentence]].

Pope [[person:nicholas-i]] sent legates to inquire. At Constantinople they sided with Photius, and Nicholas disowned them. A synod at [[place:rome]] deposed one of the legates, declared Photius stripped of every priestly office and threatened him with anathema if he continued to occupy the see or to hinder Ignatius [[cite:legates]] [[cite:sentence]]. The pope claimed, in effect, the right to judge who was patriarch of Constantinople.

The quarrel widened when the Bulgarian ruler Boris, baptized from Constantinople, turned to Rome. In 866 Nicholas answered his questions, held out the prospect of an archbishop, and told him that Constantinople ranked as a patriarchate by the favour of emperors and not by apostolic foundation [[cite:bulgars]]. Latin clergy began to work in Bulgaria. In 867 Photius wrote to the eastern patriarchs denouncing what they taught there, among other things fasting on Saturdays, the rejection of married priests, a second anointing by bishops, and the addition to the creed that the Holy Spirit proceeds also from the Son, which he held to bring two sources into the Trinity. He asked the patriarchs to send representatives to a council [[cite:encyclical]]. Nicholas, for his part, listed the same Greek charges in a letter to the Frankish bishops [[cite:charges]]. Photius's council, according to the council that afterwards condemned it and burnt its records, pronounced the pope deposed and anathematized [[cite:council]].

Neither sentence stood. A new emperor, Basil I, removed Photius and recalled Ignatius, and a council at Constantinople in 869–870, with legates of Pope Hadrian II, anathematized Photius, declared his ordinations void and burnt the records of his council [[cite:council]]. Within ten years Photius was patriarch again, and Pope John VIII was writing to the emperors that the Roman see may loose what a synod has bound, as it had for Athanasius and John Chrysostom [[cite:john]].

The two churches remembered the affair differently. Rome in 1053 still recalled Nicholas's stand against the "neophyte" Photius [[cite:leo]]. The Orthodox Church commemorates Ignatius and Photius together among its holy patriarchs [[cite:synodikon]]. The dispute had joined three questions that would return in [[event:east-west-schism]]: the pope's authority over other patriarchs, the control of newly converted peoples, and the wording of the creed.
