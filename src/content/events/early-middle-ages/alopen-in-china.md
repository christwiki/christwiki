The story of how the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire (see [[event:synod-of-seleucia-ctesiphon]]), came to China is told on a stone slab that its clergy set up in 781 [[cite:syriac]]. The slab calls their teaching *Jingjiao*, the "luminous religion" [[cite:teaching]].

According to the stele, a "most-virtuous" man named Aluoben, in older books Alopen or Olopun, came from the land it calls Da Qin, carrying sacred books. He reached [[place:changan|Chang'an]], the Tang capital, in 635. The emperor [[person:taizong]] sent his minister Fang Xuanling out to the west to meet him and bring him in. The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor inquired into the teaching of [[person:alopen|Aluoben]] in his private rooms [[cite:arrival]].

In the seventh month of 638 the emperor issued an edict, which the stele quotes. It begins:

> Right principles have no invariable name, holy men have no invariable station.

It says that Aluoben has brought books and images to the capital, that the teaching has been examined and found good for living things and for people, and that it is to be practised throughout the empire. A monastery was to be built in the Yining ward of the capital, with twenty-one monks [[cite:edict]]. A collection of Tang state documents completed in 961 preserves the same edict under the same date, in slightly different words. There Aluoben is a "Persian monk", and a further edict of 745 orders the "Persian monasteries" renamed after Da Qin, because the teaching had come from there in the first place [[cite:huiyao]].

The stele opens with a summary of the faith: one God, three in one, who made the world; a Messiah born of a virgin in Da Qin; twenty-seven sacred books; baptism with water; the sign of the cross; prayer facing east, seven times a day [[cite:teaching]]. It goes on to say that under the next emperor monasteries were founded in every prefecture, that Buddhists spoke against the teaching in 699 and that it was mocked in the capital in 713, and that the emperor Xuanzong restored it to favour [[cite:later]].

The stone is dated twice: in Chinese to the second year of Jianzhong, 781, and in Syriac to the year 1092 of the Greeks, in the time of the patriarch of the Church of the East. The priest who set it up was the son of a priest from Balkh [[cite:syriac]]. It shows a church that had come to China from Persia, not from Rome or Constantinople, and that lived there under imperial licence for a century and a half.
