[[person:william-i-of-aquitaine]], who styles himself "count and duke by the grace of God", explains his gift in the charter's opening lines. Wealth, he says, can be used to earn an eternal reward. He cannot himself despise the world, but he can support at his own cost those who do, and so share in their reward [[cite:charter]].

He therefore hands over to the apostles Peter and Paul his estate of [[place:cluny]] in the county of Mâcon, with its church, its dependants, vineyards, fields, woods, waters and mills. The gift is made for the souls of his lord King Odo, of his parents, of himself and his wife Ingelberga, of the Ava who had left him the property, and of all the faithful. A monastery is to be built there in honour of Peter and Paul, and its monks are to live by the Rule of Benedict [[cite:charter]].

The conditions are what set the charter apart. [[person:berno-of-cluny]] is to be abbot for life; after him the monks are to choose their abbot freely, according to the Rule [[cite:rule]], without interference from William's family or anyone else. Every five years they are to pay ten shillings to the church of the apostles at Rome for its lights, and they are to have the protection of the apostles and the defence of the pope. They are to care daily for the poor, strangers and pilgrims. From that day they are to be subject neither to William nor to his kin, nor to the king, nor to any earthly power; and no prince, count or bishop, not even the bishop of Rome, may seize their property, give it away or set a superior over them against their will. Anyone who tries is threatened with the fate of Dathan, Abiram and Judas and with a fine of a hundred pounds of gold. The act was done publicly at Bourges [[cite:charter]].

William thus gave up, for himself and his kin, the lordship over the house he had founded, and placed it under a distant protector [[cite:charter]]. The charter says nothing about how the monks would use this freedom. Its two provisions, free election of the abbot and freedom from every outside lord, are what it is remembered for; free election was a demand of the reformers of the following century ([[event:investiture-controversy]]).
