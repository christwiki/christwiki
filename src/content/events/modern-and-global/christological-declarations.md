The Council of Chalcedon in 451 confessed Christ "in two natures, unconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably" [[cite:chalcedon]]. The churches now called Oriental Orthodox, among them the Coptic, Syrian, Armenian and Ethiopian churches [[cite:members]], accept the first three ecumenical councils, which do not include Chalcedon [[cite:s1990-7]]. The Assyrian Church of the East professes the councils of Nicaea and Constantinople [[cite:assyrian]]. See [[event:council-of-chalcedon]], [[event:council-of-ephesus]] and [[event:synod-of-seleucia-ctesiphon]].

On 10 May 1973, at the Vatican, [[person:paul-vi]] and [[person:shenouda-iii]], Pope of Alexandria and Patriarch of the See of St Mark, signed a common declaration [[cite:d1973-head]]. In conformity with the first three ecumenical councils, it confesses that Jesus Christ is perfect God with respect to his divinity and perfect man with respect to his humanity, and that divinity and humanity are united in him without mingling, commixtion, confusion, alteration, division or separation. It speaks neither of one nature nor of two [[cite:d1973-3]]. It goes on to say that theological differences have existed since 451 and cannot be ignored, sets up a joint commission to study them, and describes the two churches as still searching for perfect communion [[cite:d1973-7]].

The Eastern Orthodox, who receive Chalcedon, held their own dialogue with the Oriental Orthodox. A joint commission meeting in Egypt in 1989 said that both families found common ground in the formula of [[person:cyril-of-alexandria]] and held the four adverbs in common, and that those who speak of two natures and those who speak of one united nature do not deny what the other affirms [[cite:s1989]]. Its second statement, in 1990, allowed each family its own terms and recorded that both accept the first three councils, while the Oriental Orthodox treat the Orthodox reading of the later ones as the Orthodox churches' own interpretation [[cite:s1990-7]]. It recommended that the churches lift the old anathemas, and submitted this to them for consideration and action [[cite:s1990-10]].

On 11 November 1994 [[person:john-paul-ii]] and Mar [[person:dinkha-iv]], Catholicos-Patriarch of the Assyrian Church of the East, signed a Common Christological Declaration at St Peter's in [[place:rome]] [[cite:d1994-1]]. It confesses Christ as true God and true man, his divinity and humanity united in one person without confusion or change, division or separation, with the difference of the natures preserved. The Assyrian church prays to Mary as Mother of Christ our God and Saviour, and the Catholic tradition calls her Mother of God and also Mother of Christ; each side recognises the other's expression as legitimate [[cite:d1994-christ]]. The old controversies, it says, led to anathemas on persons and formulas, and the divisions were due in large part to misunderstandings [[cite:d1994-past]].

The declaration is explicit about its limit. The two churches can recognise each other as sister churches, but because unanimity on the faith, the sacraments and the constitution of the church has not been reached, they cannot yet celebrate the Eucharist together; a mixed committee is set up to continue the work [[cite:d1994-church]]. Eighteen years later [[person:benedict-xvi]], writing to Mar Dinkha, still named the re-establishment of full communion as the goal of that dialogue [[cite:benedict-2012]].
