  December 1945 is the date given by James M. Robinson, who questioned villagers in the 1970s and tied the find to the season when they dug fertiliser and to a murder in the finder's family in May 1945 [[cite:robinson]]. His account is the source of the usual story, and it is late testimony. Mark Goodacre, "How Reliable is the Story of the Nag Hammadi Discovery?", *Journal for the Study of the New Testament* 35 (2013), pp. 303–322, shows that Robinson's successive versions conflict and prefers the earlier account published by Jean Doresse in 1958. Nicola Denzey Lewis and Justine Ariel Blount, "Rethinking the Origins of the Nag Hammadi Codices", *Journal of Biblical Literature* 133 (2014), pp. 399–419, doubt the story as a whole and suggest that the books were grave goods of private owners. They also dispute Robinson's proposal that monks buried the books after Athanasius's letter of 367.

In October 1946 the Coptic Museum in Cairo bought a papyrus book from a man of the district of [[place:nag-hammadi]] [[cite:find]]. A year later its director, Togo Mina, showed it to the French scholar Jean Doresse, who recognised a collection of Gnostic writings in Coptic, among them a Secret Book of John [[cite:purchase]]. Doresse and Henri-Charles Puech judged the handwriting to be no later than the second half of the fourth century [[cite:hand]]. In their report, printed in 1948, they knew of at least five volumes from the same find, which they placed at the foot of a hill near Nag Hammadi [[cite:find]].

How the books were found was already uncertain. The report repeats what was being said: that peasants had chanced on a tomb with a jar full of bound manuscripts, that two had been burned and the rest sold cheaply to dealers. Its authors ask how far the story of the tomb can be trusted [[cite:find]]. The account usually told today comes from James M. Robinson, who interviewed villagers some thirty years later. In it Muhammad Ali, of the al-Samman clan, and his brother were digging for fertiliser under the cliff of Jabal al-Tarif in December when they came on a jar, and Muhammad Ali broke it open and carried the books home [[cite:robinson]]. The archive of Robinson's project keeps photographs, dated 1975, of the man and of the cliff [[cite:photo-ali]] [[cite:photo-cliff]].

The codices hold over fifty texts and are kept in the Coptic Museum [[cite:archive]]. Codex II contains the Gospel according to Thomas, whose title stands at its end [[cite:thomas-title]]. It begins: "These are the hidden sayings that the living Jesus spoke and Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down" [[cite:thomas-prologue]]. Some of its sayings resemble those of the New Testament: asked when the kingdom will come, Jesus answers that it is already spread out over the earth and people do not see it [[cite:thomas-113]], which may be compared with [[bible:Luke 17:20-21]]. Another volume held a work called the Gospel of Truth, and Puech and Doresse asked at once whether this was the book of that name which [[person:irenaeus]], about 180, says the followers of Valentinus had written [[cite:find]] [[cite:irenaeus]].

Until then, the report observes, such groups were known mainly from the polemical notices of their opponents and from three Coptic manuscripts made known in the nineteenth century; writings that came from the groups themselves were almost wholly lacking [[cite:before]]. In his Easter letter of 367 [[person:athanasius]] had warned the churches of Egypt against books "called apocryphal", which he said heretics wrote and passed off as ancient [[cite:athanasius]]. Whether that letter had anything to do with the burial of these books is disputed; see the dating note. See also [[event:irenaeus-against-heresies]] and [[event:athanasius-festal-letter]].
