In 1943 the deaconess [[person:florence-li-tim-oi]] was in charge of an Anglican congregation in [[place:macau]], and the war kept her bishop, [[person:ronald-hall]] of Hong Kong, from reaching her. In June of that year Hall wrote to William Temple, Archbishop of Canterbury, that he had given her permission to celebrate the Lord's Supper for a congregation of 150 and would ordain her priest if he could reach her. He added that he was no advocate of the ordination of women, but would not let prejudice keep the sacraments from his people [[cite:hall-1943]].

In January 1944 she crossed Japanese-held territory to meet him at Xingxing, in unoccupied China. From there, by her own later account, they went the short distance to [[place:zhaoqing]], where he ordained her in a small Anglican church; she speaks of herself there as the first woman to become a priest [[cite:zhaoqing]]. Two days later Hall told Temple that he had ordained a Chinese deaconess "a priest in the Church of God". He had acted, he wrote, outside the canons of the Chinese church, which made no such provision; he did not expect her orders to be recognised for the present, and he would report to the General Synod after the war [[cite:hall-1944]].

The Chinese House of Bishops, meeting at Shanghai in March 1946, voted its regret at Hall's "uncanonical action" and asked him to accept the resignation from her priestly ministry which, it understood, she had already placed in his hands [[cite:bishops-1946]]. In April 1948 an Anglican group in England cited the ordination in a memorial to the Lambeth Conference [[cite:memorial]]. The Lambeth Conference of that year had before it a proposal from the diocese of South China that for twenty years a deaconess might be ordained priest as an experiment. It answered that this would be against Anglican tradition and order [[cite:lambeth-113]], reaffirmed that the order of deaconess was the one order of ministry it could recommend for women [[cite:lambeth-114]], and judged that the time had not come to reconsider the matter [[cite:lambeth-115]].

Twenty years later the bishops called the theological arguments for and against inconclusive [[cite:lambeth-34]] and asked every province to study the question [[cite:lambeth-35]]. In 1978 they noted that Hong Kong, Canada, the United States and New Zealand had admitted women to the presbyterate, recognised the right of each member church to decide, and declared their acceptance both of churches that ordain women and of churches that do not [[cite:lambeth-1978]].

John Paul II declared in 1994 that the Church has "no authority whatsoever" to confer priestly ordination on women and that this judgment is to be held definitively [[cite:rome]]. The Orthodox members of the joint commission with the Anglicans stated in 1978 that women cannot be admitted to the priesthood and that their ordination is an innovation without basis in Holy Tradition [[cite:orthodox]].
