[[person:simon-kimbangu]] belonged to the Baptist church at Wathen, a station of the English Baptist mission in the Lower Congo [[cite:lerrigo-270]]; a Kimbanguist account of 2004 calls him a former catechist of that mission [[cite:church]]. The story of his call, as the American mission secretary P. H. J. Lerrigo heard it in 1921 from a man who had it from Kimbangu, is that a stranger came to him in dreams, gave him a Bible and told him to preach and to heal, and that he refused more than once [[cite:lerrigo-270]]. At last he was told that his soul would be required of him if he did not go to a sick child in a nearby village; he went, prayed, laid his hands on the child and shook violently, and the child recovered [[cite:lerrigo-270]].

Kimbangu settled in his own village of [[place:nkamba]], and people came from every direction with their sick, by trail and by rail, so that the medical work of the missions came to a standstill [[cite:lerrigo-272]]. Lerrigo, who travelled through the district that August, records cases in which no cure followed and regarded the movement as a delusion [[cite:lerrigo-272]] [[cite:lerrigo-276]]. The Kimbanguist account says that Kimbangu preached repentance, the commandments of God and the equality of all peoples, opposed fetishes, sorcery and polygamy, and was the envoy of Jesus Christ and not his equal [[cite:church]].

Lerrigo reports talk that God was about to drive the whites out of the Congo, says that some of the movement's prophets were said to have told people not to work for them or pay taxes, and writes that the teaching that the Lord would return at once, so that planting was useless, led the government to act [[cite:lerrigo-275]]. In the Kimbanguist account Kimbangu told his judge that he had raised no revolt and had only preached the gospel [[cite:church]]. According to Jules Chomé, a Belgian lawyer who wrote a book on the trial in 1959, a court-martial at Thysville consisting of a single officer tried him without a defender [[cite:chome]]. It found that by spreading false reports of healings and resurrections and presenting himself as one sent by God he had alarmed the native population and gravely disturbed public order, and it sentenced him to death, though the prosecutor had asked for penal servitude for life [[cite:chome]]. Lerrigo thought the sentences out of proportion to the facts brought out at the trial, and reports that missionaries petitioned King Albert, who reprieved Kimbangu [[cite:lerrigo-276]]. He was held in the prison at Elisabethville until his death thirty years later [[cite:chome]].

Followers of his were scattered to places of banishment across the colony [[cite:church-12]]. At the end of 1959 the colonial administration allowed the movement to exist as a church, and in the 1960s it was led by Kimbangu's son Joseph Diangienda [[cite:chome]] [[cite:church-12]]. The church became a member of the World Council of Churches; in June 2021 the council's central committee ended that membership "for theological reasons", which its published summary does not specify [[cite:wcc]].
