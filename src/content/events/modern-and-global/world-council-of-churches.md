In January 1920 the Ecumenical Patriarchate wrote to "the Churches of Christ everywhere" that differences of doctrine need not prevent the churches from drawing closer. Pointing to the League of Nations, it proposed a league, or fellowship, of churches [[cite:encyclical]]. The World Council's own report traces its ancestry to the missionary conference at Edinburgh in 1910, on which see [[event:edinburgh-1910]], and names two further movements that flowed into it, Life and Work and Faith and Order [[cite:origins]]. A conference at Utrecht in 1938 drafted a provisional constitution and set up a provisional committee to carry on the work [[cite:utrecht]].

The first assembly met in [[place:amsterdam]]. On Monday 23 August 1948, in the Concertgebouw, with [[person:geoffrey-fisher]], Archbishop of Canterbury, in the chair, Pastor Marc Boegner moved that the assembly be declared constituted and the formation of the World Council of Churches completed. The motion was adopted with no vote against, and the archbishop declared the council formed [[cite:constituted]]. The report counts 147 churches from 44 countries, represented by 351 delegates [[cite:statistics]].

The constitution defines the council as "a fellowship of churches which accept our Lord Jesus Christ as God and Saviour" [[cite:basis]]. [[person:willem-visser-t-hooft]], its general secretary, told the assembly that it was a council of churches and not the council of the one undivided Church: an emergency solution and a stage on the road [[cite:secretary]]. The assembly's message to the churches says, "We intend to stay together" [[cite:message]].

The Roman Catholic Church was not invited to send delegates, and in June 1948 the Holy Office refused permission for any Catholic to attend as an observer. The Church of Russia was absent too, and the general secretary asked that the door be kept open for it and for other Orthodox churches not yet represented [[cite:rome]]. Pius XI had laid down in 1928 that the Apostolic See could not take part in such assemblies, because the union of Christians could be promoted only by the return of the separated to the one true Church [[cite:mortalium]].

In 1950 the council's central committee, meeting at Toronto, said what membership did and did not imply. The council "is not and must never become a superchurch", and a member church does not thereby treat its own conception of the Church as merely relative [[cite:toronto-not]]. Nor does membership oblige a church to regard the other members as churches in the true and full sense of the word [[cite:toronto-members]].
