The conference was a meeting of delegates from missionary societies of differing scope and purpose, and its organisers therefore set limits: it would deal only with missionary work among non-Christian peoples, and it would seek no opinion on any question of doctrine or church order on which those taking part differed [[cite:rules]]. British and American societies each sent rather more than 500 delegates and Continental societies over 170 [[cite:delegates]]. The record says that they stood for nearly every type of doctrine and polity except the Roman Catholic and Greek churches, and that members of the "young churches of the East" came from Japan, Korea, China, Assam, Burma, India and Ceylon [[cite:delegates]].

It opened on 14 June 1910 in the Assembly Hall of the United Free Church of Scotland in [[place:edinburgh]] [[cite:hall]] [[cite:opening]]. Lord Balfour of Burleigh, in his opening address that evening, spoke of about 1,200 members from 160 churches and organisations [[cite:balfour]]. [[person:john-mott]] was chosen to chair the day sessions, at which the reports of eight commissions were debated [[cite:opening]]. The first report, from the commission of which he was chairman [[cite:commissions]], began by saying that the church faced, as in no earlier generation, a world-wide opportunity to make Christ known [[cite:commission1]].

[[person:v-s-azariah]] of south India told an evening meeting that relations between foreign missionaries and Indian workers were too often not what they should be, and ended: "You have given your goods to feed the poor. You have given your bodies to be burned. We also ask for love. Give us FRIENDS!" [[cite:azariah]]. [[person:cheng-jingyi]], speaking for the Chinese church in the debate on unity, said that Chinese Christians hoped to see "a united Christian Church without any denominational distinctions" [[cite:cheng]].

The report on co-operation held that the ideal of missionary work was to plant in every non-Christian nation "one united Church of Christ" [[cite:commission8]]. On 21 June the conference voted unanimously to appoint a Continuation Committee, international and interdenominational, to carry on its work on the lines of the conference itself, which did not involve the idea of organic and ecclesiastical union, and to confer about a permanent international missionary committee [[cite:resolution]]. Cheng was the member named from China [[cite:members]], and the committee elected Mott as its chairman [[cite:chairman]]. The conference closed on 23 June [[cite:closing]].

The World Council of Churches counts the Edinburgh conference among its historical roots [[cite:wcc]]; see [[event:world-council-of-churches]].
