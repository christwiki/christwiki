On 24 August 1968 [[person:paul-vi]], on what he called the first personal visit of a pope to Latin America, opened the second general conference of the continent's bishops in Bogotá [[cite:homily-opening]]. He told them that the church could not stand with structures that hid and favoured grave and oppressive inequalities. He also said that neither hatred nor violence was the strength of Christian charity, and he ruled out atheistic Marxism, systematic rebellion and bloodshed as paths to social renewal [[cite:homily-social]].

The conference's conclusions, sixteen documents named for [[place:medellin]], set out to read the transformation of Latin America in the light of [[event:second-vatican-council|the Second Vatican Council]]. The introduction speaks of a continent longing for liberation from every servitude and compares its hope to Israel's deliverance from Egypt [[cite:med-intro]]. The document on justice calls the misery of whole groups an injustice that cries to heaven. It says that God sent his Son to free all people from the slaveries to which sin subjects them, among them hunger, misery and oppression, and that what is distinctive in the Christian message is not directly its call to change structures but its insistence on conversion, which then demands that change [[cite:med-justice]]. The document on peace says that in many places Latin America lives in a situation of injustice that can be called institutionalised violence, and also, in words it marks as a quotation, that violence is neither Christian nor evangelical [[cite:med-peace]]. The document on the poverty of the church distinguishes poverty as want, which is an evil, from spiritual poverty and from poverty freely taken up in solidarity, and asks that the church's efforts give effective preference to the poorest [[cite:med-poverty]].

The theologian [[person:gustavo-gutierrez]] of Lima, who had been closely involved in the conference [[cite:gut-life]], took up the word in an article of 1970, "Notes for a Theology of Liberation", which cites the Medellín documents repeatedly. Theology, he wrote, is critical reflection on the church's presence and action in the world; it comes second, after commitment [[cite:gut-244]]. "Liberation" was a better word than "development" for peoples who were poor because they were dependent and oppressed. It had three levels: the political liberation of oppressed peoples and classes, humanity's liberation through history, and liberation from sin [[cite:gut-247]]. To do nothing for the oppressed, he argued, was to act against them [[cite:gut-254]]. The poor of his day were the oppressed and marginal, and the church could preach spiritual poverty only by making itself poor in protest against poverty [[cite:gut-260]].

In 1984 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith under Cardinal [[person:benedict-xvi|Joseph Ratzinger]], with the approval of [[person:john-paul-ii]], issued an instruction on the subject [[cite:ln-end]]. It called "theology of liberation" a valid expression [[cite:ln-3]] and said its purpose was limited: to warn against certain forms of that theology which used concepts borrowed from Marxist thought without enough criticism. This, it said, was no disavowal of those who respond to the preferential option for the poor [[cite:ln-intro]]. Its objections were that such theologies took class struggle as the law of history, merged the history of salvation with secular history, and confused the poor of Scripture with Marx's proletariat. It speaks of "theologians of liberation" in general and names none [[cite:ln-9]].

A second instruction followed in 1986, to be read together with the first [[cite:lc-2]]. It affirms the church's love of preference for the poor [[cite:lc-68]], rejects class struggle as a theory and what it calls the myth of revolution [[cite:lc-77]], and says that a theology of freedom and liberation is needed [[cite:lc-98]].
