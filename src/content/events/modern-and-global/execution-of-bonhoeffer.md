  The West German courts that tried the SS prosecutor after the war found that the five prisoners were hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945 between six and seven in the morning [[cite:court]]. The editor of the prison letters, in his foreword dated August 1951, gives the same day [[cite:foreword]].

  A description of Bonhoeffer's last minutes, kneeling in prayer and dying within seconds, is often quoted. It comes from a statement written in 1955 by the camp doctor, H. Fischer-Hüllstrung. Ferdinand Schlingensiepen, *Dietrich Bonhoeffer 1906–1945: Martyr, Thinker, Man of Resistance* (2010), calls the doctor's report "unfortunately a lie" and sets against it the testimony of a Danish prisoner of the camp. The appendix to the prison letters repeats one detail of it, that the doctor saw Bonhoeffer kneeling in prayer [[cite:last-days]]. It is not used here.

In 1937 [[person:dietrich-bonhoeffer]] published *Nachfolge*, known in English as *The Cost of Discipleship*. Its first chapter calls "cheap grace" the deadly enemy of the church: a grace that justifies the sin and not the sinner, so that everything can stay as it was. Grace, he wrote, is costly because it calls a person to follow Jesus Christ [[cite:nachfolge]]. In prison he remembered Finkenwalde, one of the places in Pomerania where the Confessing Church kept its seminary for preachers [[cite:seminary]]. On that church see [[event:barmen-declaration]].

He was arrested on 5 April 1943 and spent the next eighteen months in the military section of Tegel prison in [[place:berlin]], as Eberhard Bethge, his friend and the editor of his letters, records [[cite:foreword]]. A German court later found that he was held as a member of the resistance circle in military intelligence [[cite:court]]. From Tegel he wrote to Bethge on 30 April 1944 that the question which never left him was who Christ really is for us today, and that a time without religion was coming [[cite:letter-april]]. On 21 July 1944 he wrote that he still stood by *Nachfolge* but now saw its dangers, and that a person learns to believe only by living fully in this world and taking seriously the suffering of God in it [[cite:letter-july]].

After files of the conspirators were found at Zossen in September 1944 the Gestapo moved him to its own prison in Berlin, and from there, as his family later learned, to Buchenwald and Schönberg [[cite:foreword]]. On Sunday 8 April 1945 he led a service for his fellow prisoners at Schönberg and was then called away. The English prisoner Payne Best, who was among them, reported that Bonhoeffer gave him a greeting for the Bishop of Chichester, with the words that this was the end, but for him the beginning of life [[cite:last-days]].

The court found that he cannot have reached [[place:flossenburg]] concentration camp before the late afternoon of that day [[cite:court]]. There an SS judge, sitting with the camp commandant and one other, tried him and four others: Admiral Canaris, General Oster, the army judge Sack and Captain Gehre [[cite:court]] [[cite:last-days]]. The appendix to the letters names a sixth man, Strünk, among those heard [[cite:last-days]]. No defence counsel was appointed and no clerk was present to keep a record. All five were sentenced to death, and on the morning of 9 April they were hanged, naked, before any confirmation of the sentences had been obtained [[cite:court]]. The court held that the law required such confirmation in every case [[cite:court-law]].

Six months later the council of the Evangelical Church in Germany, meeting at Stuttgart, declared itself bound to the German people in a "solidarity of guilt" and accused itself of not having confessed more courageously or prayed more faithfully [[cite:stuttgart]]. The church's own account says that the document was addressed to the Bishop of Chichester, George Bell, whom it calls Bonhoeffer's friend [[cite:stuttgart-intro]]. Bethge's edition of the letters from prison followed in 1951 [[cite:foreword]].
