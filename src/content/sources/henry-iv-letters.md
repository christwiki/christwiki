Two letters of the king to "Hildebrand" survive from the breach of 1076. Carl Erdmann prints them as nos. 11 and 12 of his edition, assigns both simply to 1076, and says in his introduction that all of Henry's letters are undated.

The shorter (no. 11) tells [[person:gregory-vii]] that an assembly of the chief men of the kingdom has found that he cannot remain in the apostolic see, refers him to the bishops' own letter, and orders him to come down from the see of Rome. It is the letter that Henry passed on to the clergy and people of Rome in a letter of his own (no. 10). The chronicler Lampert of Hersfeld puts the assembly at Worms on Septuagesima Sunday, which in 1076 was 24 January.

The longer (no. 12) is the one Henderson translates, and the one meant where an entry cites the letter "to Hildebrand" without a number. It argues that a king anointed by God is to be judged by God alone and may be deposed only for heresy, and it accuses Gregory of having gained the papacy by money and violence. "Jan. 24, 1076" is Henderson's heading, the date of the assembly at Worms; the text carries no date. Gerold Meyer von Knonau, *Jahrbücher des Deutschen Reiches unter Heinrich IV. und Heinrich V.*, vol. 2 (1894), pp. 662–663, following Carl Mirbt, held it most probable that this letter went out from Utrecht at Easter 1076, after Henry had learned of Gregory's sentence on him, as a sharper version of the letter sent from Worms; he notes that the chronicler Bruno gives it with the Worms letters. Henderson's closing words, "to be damned throughout the ages", render a reading that Erdmann reports from the Codex Udalrici only and does not take into his text, which ends "Descend, descend!"

Both letters are polemic, and Erdmann's apparatus shows that they come down through the chronicle of Bruno and through letter collections, not as originals. They should be read as statements of the royal case, not as reports of facts.
