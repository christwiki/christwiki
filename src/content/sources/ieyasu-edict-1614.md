The proclamation begins with a statement of the order of things: Japan is the land of the gods and of Buddha, whose teaching came from China and whose principles of benevolence and right conduct govern the country. Against this it sets "the Kirishitan band", who have come not only to trade but to spread "an evil law" and to "change the government of the country, and obtain possession of the land". It charges that Christians honour condemned criminals, and it orders that they be "instantly swept out, so that not an inch of soil remains to them in Japan on which to plant their feet".

Satow, who translated it, says that it was published on 27 January 1614, in the twelfth month of the eighteenth year of Keichō, and prints it under the heading "Proclamation of Iyeyasu". James Murdoch, *A History of Japan*, vol. 2 (1903), says that it appeared under the name of Hidetada, Ieyasu's son. Nathalie Kouamé, "Sûden's Anti-Christian Edict (The) (1614)", in *Encyclopédie des historiographies: Afriques, Amériques, Asies*, vol. 1 (2020), pp. 1760–1779, reports that the Zen monk Ishin Sūden wrote the text at Ieyasu's order and kept it in his register of foreign affairs; that the register dates the order to the evening of the 22nd day of the twelfth month, 31 January 1614 by her conversion; and that the shogun Hidetada signed it. Satow's day falls four days earlier and is given here as his. The text states the government's reasons in its own terms.

Satow adds that the proclamation was accompanied by fifteen rules for the Buddhist priests who were to vouch for the religion of their parishioners, and prints them after it. Tamamuro Fumio, "Local Society and the Temple-Parishioner Relationship within the Bakufu's Governance Structure", *Japanese Journal of Religious Studies* 28/3–4 (2001), pp. 265–266, identifies these rules, which carry the date 1613, as a forgery made no earlier than about 1735: they name groups that were banned only in 1669 and 1691. They are not cited here as part of the proclamation.
