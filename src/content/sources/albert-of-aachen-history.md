Little is known of Albert. A. C. Krey, introducing his translation in 1921, says that Albert by his own admission never visited the Holy Land, that he drew on the spoken and written testimony of those who had, and that his work, which Krey has running to 1120, had been dated at some point between that year and the middle of the century. Krey adds that its value has been much disputed.

More recent scholarship dates the work earlier than Krey did, and disagrees about how much earlier. Susan B. Edgington, who edited the Latin text (*Albert of Aachen: Historia Ierosolimitana*, Oxford Medieval Texts, 2007), sets out her dating in the introduction to her translation (*Albert of Aachen's History of the Journey to Jerusalem*, vol. 2, 2013, pp. 2–3). The prologue, she argues, shows that Albert first meant to write the history of the crusade alone, which is books 1–6, and these "were written soon after the events they describe". Books 7–12 run to 1119, so the whole cannot have been finished before that year, and she thinks it probably the twelve-book work on the journey to Jerusalem listed in the catalogue of the abbey of Egmond in 1128–30. Vedran Sulovsky, "A crusader in the twelfth-century imperial court chapel: identifying Albert of Aachen as Albert of Sponheim", *Crusades* 24 (2025), pp. 20–46, rejects the early date for the first six books: he finds no evidence that any of the work was written about 1102 and holds that Albert wrote all of it between 1119 and 1130.

His account of the massacres at Cologne and Mainz, which stands in book 1, is that of a Christian cleric of the region. On Edgington's dating he wrote it in the first decade of the twelfth century, soon after the events of 1096; on Sulovsky's, a generation after them. He condemns the killing and says that the attackers acted from greed. Hebrew accounts from the Jewish communities themselves also survive; one, on Mainz, can be read in translation at the Fordham Sourcebook.
