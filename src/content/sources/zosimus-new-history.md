Zosimus wrote about the year 500, nearly two centuries after [[person:constantine]], and for this period is thought to follow the lost history of the pagan Eunapius. The Byzantine scholar Photius, who read him in the ninth century, calls him a count and former advocate of the treasury, and "an impious heathen". His thesis is that the empire fell into ruin because it gave up the ancestral rites, and he is as hostile to Constantine as Eusebius is admiring.

That hostility is what makes him useful beside the Christian writers. His account of the war with [[person:maxentius]] in book 2 has the same outline as theirs, the march into Italy, the battle outside Rome, the collapse of the bridge and the tyrant's drowning, but says nothing of a vision or a sign. He wrote long after the events, and his very large figures for the two armies have no other support.
