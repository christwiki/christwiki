The collection that goes under Julian's name includes private letters to philosophers and friends, public letters to cities, and instructions to officials and priests.

For the religious policy of his reign the genuine letters are the primary evidence, since they come from the emperor himself. Cited here are the rescript on Christian teachers (36), which explains the law of 17 June 362, and the letter to the citizens of Bostra (41), dated 1 August 362, in which he says that he has recalled the bishops exiled by Constantius and forces no one to sacrifice. They show the policy as Julian wished it to be seen. How it was applied in the provinces is known mainly from Christian writers, who were hostile to him.

The letter to Arsacius, high priest of Galatia (22; number 84 in Bidez's edition), on reforming the pagan priesthood and founding hostels, is also cited, and it stands apart. Wright's note on it says that it "is not extant in any MS. of Julian" and is quoted in full by the church historian [[source:sozomen-church-history|Sozomen]] (Church History 5.16), who wrote about eighty years after Julian's death and introduces it as "a letter written by the emperor himself". Its authenticity is disputed. Peter Van Nuffelen, "Deux fausses lettres de Julien l'Apostat (la lettre aux Juifs, Ep. 51 [Wright], et la lettre à Arsacius, Ep. 84 [Bidez])", *Vigiliae Christianae* 56 (2002), pp. 131–150, says that its authenticity had not been questioned before and argues that it betrays the hand of a Christian of the first half of the fifth century. Jean Bouffartigue, "L'authenticité de la Lettre 84 de l'empereur Julien", *Revue de philologie, de littérature et d'histoire anciennes* 79 (2005), pp. 231–242, answers his arguments and concludes that it is prudent to go on treating the letter as genuine.
