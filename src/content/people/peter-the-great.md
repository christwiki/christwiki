The manifesto that opens the Spiritual Regulation is issued in his name as Peter the First, tsar and sovereign of all Russia. In it he says that, having reformed the military and civil orders, he fears to seem ungrateful to God if he neglects the spiritual order, and that, after the example of pious kings in the Old and New Testaments, he takes its regulation upon himself [[cite:manifesto]]. The members of the college he set up swore to be faithful to him and his heirs and acknowledged him as its supreme judge [[cite:oath]].

Thomas Consett, who translated the Regulation into English, wrote that the tsar had kept the patriarchate vacant after the death of Patriarch Adrian because he thought that a patriarch's power would obstruct his designs [[cite:consett]]. The patriarch of Constantinople, recognising the Synod in 1723, calls him the "most pious and pacific Autocrat" [[cite:jeremias]]. See [[event:holy-synod-of-russia]].
