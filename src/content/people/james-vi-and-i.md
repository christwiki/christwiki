The translators of 1611 dedicate their work to James as king of Great Britain, France and Ireland, and speak of him as having succeeded [[person:elizabeth-i|Queen Elizabeth]] and as the one who set the translation in motion [[cite:dedication]]. William Barlow reports his words at Hampton Court in January 1604: he wanted one uniform translation, made by the universities, reviewed by the bishops and ratified by himself, and without the notes of the Geneva Bible, some of which he thought seditious [[cite:barlow]]. See [[event:king-james-bible]].

The settlers who signed the agreement at Cape Cod in November 1620 styled themselves his loyal subjects and dated it in the eighteenth year of his reign in England and the fifty-fourth in Scotland [[cite:compact]]; see [[event:pilgrims-at-plymouth]]. [[person:thomas-helwys]] told him in print that as an earthly king he had no power over his subjects' consciences [[cite:conscience]]. The Dutch synod that met at Dordrecht in 1618 says that it obtained theologians from Great Britain by his favour [[cite:dort]]; see [[event:synod-of-dort]].
