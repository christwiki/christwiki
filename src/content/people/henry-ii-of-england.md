The royal clerk Roger of Hoveden calls him throughout "king Henry, son of the empress Matilda". In 1162, which Hoveden counts as the eighth year of his reign, he made his chancellor [[person:thomas-becket]] archbishop of [[place:canterbury]], and the next year the two quarrelled over the trial of clergy accused of crimes [[cite:hov-1162]]. The Constitutions of Clarendon of 1164 present his claims as the customs of his grandfather, King Henry I [[cite:clarendon]].

After the murder he swore at Avranches, before two cardinal legates, that he had neither commanded nor wished the archbishop's death, while admitting the fear that his own anger had moved the killers; he promised to allow appeals to Rome [[cite:hov-1172]]. In July 1174, in the midst of a rebellion, he walked barefoot to the tomb at Canterbury and submitted to be scourged by the bishops, priests and monks [[cite:hov-1174]].
