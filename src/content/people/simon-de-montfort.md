After Carcassonne fell in 1209 the leaders of the crusade chose Simon de Montfort as prince and lord of the conquered land; the legates described him to the pope as a man strong in arms and devout in faith, who had at once ordered tithes to be paid to the churches and three pence a year to Rome from every hearth [[cite:chosen]]. Peter of les Vaux-de-Cernay, the crusade's historian, calls him "our count". He tells how at Minerve in 1210 Simon urged the "perfect" heretics to convert and, when they refused, had a great fire made, into which, Peter says, a hundred and forty or more threw themselves of their own accord [[cite:minerve]], and how in 1213 King Peter of Aragon fell in battle against his knights at Muret [[cite:muret]].

At the council of 1215 the pope, with the approval of the greater part of the council, awarded him Toulouse and the other lands the crusaders had taken, and the king of France invested him with them [[cite:council]]. Toulouse turned against him. Peter says that he besieged it from 1217 and that, after some nine months, on the day after the feast of John the Baptist, a stone from a mangonel struck him on the head; he calls him a martyr [[cite:death]]. Caesarius of Heisterbach, in the Rhineland, calls him a Catholic man who held the count of Toulouse's lands by right of war [[cite:caesarius]]. See [[event:albigensian-crusade]].
