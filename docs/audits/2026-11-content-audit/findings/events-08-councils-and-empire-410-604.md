# Events: Councils and Empire, 410–604

Reviewer batch E08. Severity: A = factual error, B = misleading, unsupported or inconsistent, C = minor imprecision or omission. See the [audit report](../README.md) for method and limits.

## Findings

### B · historical · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/columba-on-iona.md
- **Text:** "[[person:columba]], called Colum Cille in Irish, was a priest and abbot who had already founded the monastery of Durrow in Ireland [[cite:bede]]."
- **Problem:** Stated in the wiki's own voice on Bede alone, although the entry's other main source, Adomnán, says otherwise: he has Columba founding Durrow during a stay of some months in the Irish midlands in the course of which he visited Clonmacnoise "under the abbot Alither" (the annals make Alither abbot from 585), i.e. on a visit made from Iona, some twenty years after 563. Two cited sources disagree and one is silently chosen (a defect the authoring guide names); modern editors of Adomnán (Anderson and Anderson; Sharpe) follow Adomnán and put Durrow in the later 580s.
- **Should be:** "Bede says that before he crossed to Britain he had founded the monastery of Durrow in Ireland [[cite:bede]]; Adomnán places the founding of Durrow during a later stay in Ireland, when Alither was abbot of Clonmacnoise (Life 1.3)." Drop "already".
- **Evidence:** Adomnán, Life 1.3 (Reeves), read at https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/columba-e.asp : "while the blessed man was residing for a few months in the midland part of Hibernia, when founding by divine inspiration his monastery, which in the Scotic tongue is called Dair-mag (Durrow), was pleased to pay a visit to the brethren who dwelt in St. Ceran's monastery, Clon … under the abbot Alither". Bede 3.4 has the monastery built "before he passed over into Britain". Alither's years (585–599): Annals of Ulster, own knowledge.
- **Confidence:** high that the two sources conflict; medium on which is right

### B · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/pelagian-controversy.md
- **Text:** "Innocent's successor Zosimus at first gave Caelestius a hearing and time to clear himself [[cite:zosimus]]. … Zosimus accepted the decrees, the emperor Honorius legislated against the Pelagians"
- **Problem:** This is Augustine's deliberately gentle version, told in the wiki's own voice. Zosimus went much further: after hearing Caelestius at Rome in 417 he approved his confession of faith, wrote to the African bishops that they had acted too hastily, and after reading Pelagius's statement wrote again defending Pelagius and reproving his accusers (letters *Magnum pondus* and *Postquam a nobis*, Collectio Avellana 45–46). He reversed himself only after the African protest and the emperor's intervention. The closing sentence also inverts the order: Honorius's rescript expelling Pelagius and Caelestius is dated 30 April 418, the day before the council of 1 May, and Zosimus's circular (the *Tractoria*) followed both.
- **Should be:** "Zosimus at first received Caelestius, approved his statement of faith and that of Pelagius, and told the African bishops in two letters of 417 that they had judged in haste (Collectio Avellana 45–46); Augustine describes this as a hearing and a delay [[cite:zosimus]]. On 30 April 418 the emperor Honorius ordered Pelagius and Caelestius expelled from Rome; on 1 May the council at Carthage issued its canons; Zosimus then condemned both in a circular letter."
- **Evidence:** Catholic Encyclopedia, "Pope St. Zosimus" (https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15764c.htm), opened: Caelestius made "a confession of faith which was approved … [Zosimus] considered the action of the African bishops against Coelestius too hasty. He wrote at once in this sense to the bishops of the African province … the assembly held the statements [of Pelagius] to be orthodox, and Zosimus again wrote to the African bishops defending Pelagius and reproving his accusers". Date of the rescript: own knowledge (PL 48, 379–386); not re-opened.
- **Confidence:** high on Zosimus; medium on the rescript's exact date

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/council-of-constantinople-ii.md
- **Text:** summary "parts of the West resist for decades"; body "In the West the decision was resisted. Some forty years on, Gregory the Great was still writing to bishops who remained in schism over it"
- **Problem:** (1) "For decades" understates: the schism of Aquileia and Istria over the Three Chapters lasted until the synod of Pavia about 698, nearly a century and a half; Milan returned about 572. (2) The entry gives the readings of Rome and Constantinople only. The Church of the East, for which Theodore of Mopsuestia is "the Interpreter", never received this council and in its own synod of 585 forbade anyone to reject Theodore's writings; nothing in the entry (or elsewhere in the wiki: "Theodore of Mopsuestia" occurs in this file only) says so, although the same wiki tells the Church of the East's story at Ephesus and in China. (3) The entry never says what Vigilius's "own judgment" (the first *Constitutum*, 14 May 553) decided: it condemned sixty propositions drawn from Theodore but refused to anathematise a man who had died in the peace of the church or to condemn Theodoret and Ibas, whom Chalcedon had received. Without that, "first refuses and then accepts" has no content.
- **Should be:** Summary: "parts of the West resist, northern Italy until about 698". Add one sentence on the *Constitutum* and one, cited to the Synodicon orientale (synod of Ishoʿyahb I, 585, canon 2; Chabot's translation pp. 398–400), on the Church of the East's reception of Theodore.
- **Evidence:** Own knowledge (Paul the Deacon, *Hist. Lang.* 6.14 for Pavia; Collectio Avellana 83 for the Constitutum; Chabot for 585). Grep of `src/content` for "Theodore of Mopsuestia", "Aquileia".
- **Confidence:** high (1, 3); medium (2: canon and page from memory)

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/council-of-ephesus.md
- **Text:** "Cyril of Alexandria wrote to correct him. … The emperor Theodosius II summoned the bishops to Ephesus. … Nestorius refused to appear and was deposed 'with many tears' [[cite:synod]] [[cite:decree]]. The legates of the bishop of Rome came later and confirmed the sentence"
- **Problem:** Two things a careful reader needs are missing. (a) Rome's part before the council: Celestine's Roman synod of August 430 had already condemned Nestorius and commissioned Cyril to execute the sentence; the very decree the entry cites says the bishops acted "compelled thereto by the canons and by the letter of our most holy father and fellow-servant Cœlestine, the Roman bishop". As written, Rome appears only after the fact, which is the reading least favourable to the Catholic account. (b) Cyril's third letter with the Twelve Chapters (anathemas), which is what John of Antioch and the Easterns actually objected to and what the peace of 433 passed over in silence; the Henotikon entry later says the Henotikon "receives the twelve chapters of Cyril" without the wiki ever having said what they are. Also unmentioned is the council's ruling against composing "another faith" than Nicaea's (canon 7), invoked at Ephesus 449 and in the later filioque dispute.
- **Should be:** Add: "Celestine of Rome, with a synod in 430, had condemned Nestorius's teaching and charged Cyril to act for him; Cyril then sent Nestorius a third letter with twelve anathemas" and note that the sentence cites Celestine's letter [[cite:decree]].
- **Evidence:** Decree against Nestorius, opened at https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf214/npnf214.x.xi.html (quoted above). Rest: own knowledge.
- **Confidence:** high

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/second-council-of-ephesus.md
- **Text:** "Flavian wrote to Leo that … when he appealed to the Roman see soldiers surrounded him [[cite:appeal]]." (and nothing further on Flavian)
- **Problem:** The entry never says what became of Flavian. He died within months of the council, on the way to or in exile; at Chalcedon bishops cried out against Dioscorus and the monk Barsauma as his murderers, and his death is why Leo and Chalcedon treat him as a martyr-confessor. The wiki's own person page says only that he "was dead by 451", and the source page for the appeal that he "died not long afterwards". It is the outcome for one of the entry's five named actors and the main reason for the name "Robber Council".
- **Should be:** Add a sentence: "Flavian died soon afterwards. At Chalcedon Eusebius of Dorylaeum charged that he had been murdered, and a bishop named the monk Barsauma as the one who had cried 'Slay him' (Evagrius 2.2, 2.18)."
- **Evidence:** Evagrius book 2, opened at https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/evagrius_2_book2.htm : 2.2 "Flavian had even been brought to a miserable end by being thrust and trampled on by Dioscorus himself" (a report Evagrius relays); 2.4 and 2.18, Eusebius of Dorylaeum's petition at Chalcedon, "the bishop Flavian was murdered, and, together with myself, unjustly deposed by him"; 2.18, Diogenes of Cyzicus "stated that Barsumas … had been the murderer of Flavian, for he had exclaimed 'Slay him!'". These are the accusers' words and should be attributed as such.
- **Confidence:** high

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/council-of-chalcedon.md
- **Text:** "Canon 28 gave the see of Constantinople, as the imperial city, privileges equal to those of old Rome and the rank next after it [[cite:canon-28]]."
- **Problem:** The operative half of the canon is left out: it gives Constantinople the right to ordain the metropolitans of the dioceses of Pontus, Asia and Thrace and the bishops "among the barbarians" of those dioceses. That jurisdiction, not the honorary rank (already in canon 3 of 381), is what made a patriarchate, what Leo's legates and Leo himself attacked as an invasion of others' rights, and what is still argued over today (the "barbarian lands" clause).
- **Should be:** Add: "…and the right to ordain the metropolitans of Pontus, Asia and Thrace and the bishops of those dioceses among the barbarians."
- **Evidence:** Canon 28 in Percival, opened at the entry's own link (https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/npnf214/npnf214.xi.xviii.xxviii.html): "…and rank next after her; so that, in the Pontic, the Asian, and the Thracian dioceses, the metropolitans only and such bishops also of the Dioceses aforesaid as are among the barbarians, should be ordained by the aforesaid most holy throne of the most holy Church of Constantinople".
- **Confidence:** high

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/patrick-in-ireland.md
- **Text:** "Bede and the Irish annals both record that Pope Celestine sent Palladius as first bishop to the Irish, and Bede's notice speaks of Irish who already believed in Christ [[cite:palladius]] [[cite:annals-432]]."
- **Problem:** The notice is not Bede's. Bede (731) and the annals copy it from Prosper of Aquitaine's chronicle under 431, written in Gaul within a few years of the event: "Ad Scottos in Christum credentes ordinatus a papa Caelestino Palladius primus episcopus mittitur." It is the one contemporary, dated statement about Christianity in Ireland in Patrick's century, and the entry cites it only at second hand through sources three and more centuries later, while the dating note stresses that everything datable comes from late annals. Prosper's chronicle is already a source in the wiki (used in sack-of-rome and pelagian-controversy).
- **Should be:** "Prosper of Aquitaine, a contemporary, records under 431 that Pope Celestine sent Palladius as first bishop 'to the Irish who believe in Christ' (Prosper, Chronicle 1307); Bede and the Irish annals repeat the notice [[cite:palladius]] [[cite:annals-432]]." Mention in the dating note that this is the only firm date in the story.
- **Evidence:** Own knowledge of Prosper, *Chron.* 1307 (Mommsen, Chron. min. I p. 473, the same volume the wiki links for 1240–1266); Bede 1.13 reproduces it verbatim.
- **Confidence:** high

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/baptism-of-clovis.md
- **Text:** "Two accounts of his baptism are followed here: a letter written to him at the time by Bishop Avitus of Vienne, and the narrative of Gregory of Tours" / dating note "The one contemporary witness, the letter of Avitus"
- **Problem:** A third early witness, older than Gregory and central to the date debate the note sets out, is not mentioned: the letter of Bishop Nicetius of Trier to Clovis's granddaughter Chlodoswinda (c. 565, *Epistolae Austrasicae* 8), which says that Clovis promised to be baptised after seeing the miracles at the shrine of St Martin at Tours. Since Tours was in Visigothic hands until the war of 507 (apart from brief Frankish incursions), this is one of the main supports for the late dating, used by Shanzer and by Ian Wood, "Gregory of Tours and Clovis" (*Revue belge de philologie et d'histoire* 63, 1985). Minor: Gregory does not actually say where the baptism took place; Reims (front matter `places`) is an inference from the bishop's see.
- **Should be:** Add to the dating note: "Nicetius of Trier, writing about 565, links the king's decision with a visit to the shrine of St Martin at Tours (Epistolae Austrasicae 8), which favours a date after the Franks reached Tours."
- **Evidence:** Own knowledge; Gregory 2.31 read at sourcebooks.fordham.edu (no place named).
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/fall-of-the-western-empire.md
- **Text:** dating note "The narrative sources used here give no day." / body "Others describe a change of ruler inside an empire that continued."
- **Problem:** (a) Days do exist in the annalistic record: the Ravenna-based consular annals (Fasti Vindobonenses priores, Chron. min. I, under 476) give Odoacer's elevation as king on 23 August, the killing of Orestes at Placentia on 28 August and of his brother Paulus at Ravenna on 4 September, the day conventionally given for the deposition. (b) The strongest contemporary evidence for the "empire continued" reading is absent: Malchus (fr. 10 Müller / 14 Blockley) reports that the Roman senate, at Odoacer's prompting, sent the imperial insignia to Zeno, saying that one emperor sufficed for both parts, and that Zeno answered that Nepos was still the lawful emperor of the West.
- **Should be:** Add both, each with its source; `date` could then carry month 9 with the caveat that the day is that of Paulus's death.
- **Evidence:** Own knowledge; not re-opened in this session (check Mommsen, Chron. min. I pp. 308–311, and Blockley's Malchus).
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · completeness · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/rule-of-benedict.md
- **Text:** "Gregory adds that Benedict wrote a rule for monks … The Rule calls the monastery 'a school of the Lord's service' in which the author hopes to lay down 'nothing harsh or burdensome' [[cite:prologue]]."
- **Problem:** The entry presents the Rule's contents as Benedict's own composition and never mentions that much of the prologue and chapters 1–7 (including the "school of the Lord's service" passage, the chapter on the kinds of monks and the degrees of humility, all quoted here) is taken, often word for word, from the anonymous Rule of the Master, which most scholars since the mid-twentieth century hold to be the earlier text. The wiki's own source page `rule-of-benedict` says exactly this; the event entry and its dating note do not, so the two pages give different impressions.
- **Should be:** One sentence in the body or dating note: "Much of the prologue and of chapters 1–7 stands almost word for word in a longer anonymous rule, the Rule of the Master, which most scholars now think Benedict used (A. de Vogüé, *La Règle de saint Benoît*, Sources chrétiennes 181–186, 1971–72)."
- **Evidence:** `src/content/sources/rule-of-benedict.md` line 22; own knowledge.
- **Confidence:** high

### C · plausibility · src/content/events/councils-and-empire/third-council-of-toledo.md
- **Text:** "a joint Orthodox and Catholic statement of 2003 calls it the earliest use of such language in a creed-like text"
- **Problem:** Two small things. (a) The document is the agreed statement of the *North American* Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation, a regional dialogue of theologians appointed by the bishops of the United States and Canada, not a statement of the two churches as such; the body text should say so (the source page does). (b) The same statement, a few paragraphs later, says that the Athanasian Creed, "probably originated in Gaul about 500", "clearly affirmed that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son"; and A. Ferreiro's lexicon article, which the dating note relies on, mentions the same words in the creed transmitted with the First Council of Toledo (as a suspected interpolation). So "earliest" is the statement's wording for the creed of 381 in a conciliar profession, not for Latin creeds in general; left bare it reads as a larger claim than the document supports.
- **Should be:** "the North American Orthodox–Catholic Theological Consultation, in an agreed statement of 2003, calls it the earliest use of such language in a credal context, while noting that the Latin creed called Athanasian, of about 500, already says the same".
- **Evidence:** Statement opened at https://www.usccb.org/resources/filioque-a-church-dividing-issue.pdf ("The earliest use of Filioque language in a credal context is in the profession of faith formulated for the Visigoth King Reccared … 589"; "the so-called Athanasian Creed … probably originated in Gaul about 500 … clearly affirmed that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son"). Ferreiro's article opened at konziliengeschichte.org (database/4300): it exists, is dated January 2025 and says what the dating note reports.
- **Confidence:** high

## Completeness of this stretch

Checked against `event-index.txt` (19 events, 410–604) and by grep of `src/content` for: Orange, Attila, Vandal, Dionysius Exiguus, Theodosian Code, Code of Justinian/Corpus Iuris, Stylite, Boethius, Cassiodorus, Areopagite, Columbanus, Lombard, Dvin, Beth Lapat/Barsauma, Theodore of Mopsuestia, Nine Saints, Mar Aba/Babai, Rule of the Master, Aquileia, Cassian, Caesarius, Athanasian Creed, Brigid, Mesrop/Mashtots, Avarayr, Nisibis.

- **The Church of the East's own doctrinal path (424–486 and after)** — not covered. The wiki tells its organisation in 410 and then jumps to modern declarations (in `council-of-ephesus`) and to China in 635. Missing: the synod of Dadishoʿ (424) declaring the catholicos subject to no western appeal; the closing of the school of Edessa (489) and the school of Nisibis; the synods of 484/486 that adopted a two-nature confession and clerical marriage; the canonising of Theodore (585). All are in the Synodicon orientale, already a source. Without them the summary of `council-of-ephesus` ("long divides the Church of the East … from the churches that receive it") has no narrative behind it, and a reader would assume that church condemned or was condemned at Ephesus in 431, which it was not. Belongs as one event c. 484–486 in this era.
- **Armenia and Chalcedon** — `council-of-chalcedon` lists the Armenian church among those that do not receive the council, but nothing says when or how (councils of Dvin, 506 and 555); nor is the Armenian alphabet and Bible of Mesrop Mashtots (c. 405) covered ("Dvin", "Mesrop" → 0 files). The Ethiopian "Nine Saints" are likewise absent.
- **Second Council of Orange (529)** — absent (grep "Orange" finds only William III). It is the Western church's settlement of the grace controversy that `pelagian-controversy` leaves open with "The argument did not end there", and it was cited by both sides in the Reformation and Jansenist debates the wiki does cover. One sentence in `pelagian-controversy`, or an event of its own, with John Cassian and Prosper.
- **Leo the Great beyond the Tome** — Leo's meeting with Attila (452) and the Vandal sack of Rome (455), and the Vandal persecution of Catholics in Africa (Victor of Vita), are mentioned only in people/source pages; Augustine's death in besieged Hippo (430) likewise. Thin for the thread church-and-state between 410 and 476.
- **Justinian's legislation** — the Code, Digest and Novels (529–534), with their laws on heretics, Jews, pagans and the closing of the Athenian schools (529), appear only as the preface of Novel 6 inside `justinian-and-hagia-sophia`. The Theodosian Code (438) is named only in a person summary. Both shaped church law for a millennium.
- **The Lombard invasion of Italy (568) and Gregory's dealings with the Lombards** — absent from this era's events, though it is the setting of Gregory's pontificate (peace of 598, Theodelinda) and the premise of `donation-of-pepin`. `gregory-the-great` says nothing of his government of Rome, the papal estates or the Lombards, nor of the Dialogues and Moralia.
- **Columbanus (to Gaul c. 590; Luxeuil, Bobbio)** — 0 files. The continental Irish mission and the Easter and penitential practices it carried are a first-rank development between `columba-on-iona` and `synod-of-whitby`.
- **Dionysius Exiguus and the AD era (525)** — only named as a translator in one source page. For a timeline that dates everything "AD" this deserves at least a paragraph; his canon collection also carried the African and Eastern canons cited in this batch to the West.
- **Monastic and devotional figures of the East** — Simeon Stylites, Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, Romanos the Melodist, Sabas and the Palestinian lauras (the setting of the Origenist quarrel that `council-of-constantinople-ii` mentions through Evagrius) are all absent; Boethius and Cassiodorus's Vivarium likewise in the West.
- **Theopaschite formula / Justinian's edicts of 533 and 543–551** — the steps between the Henotikon and 553 ("one of the Trinity suffered", the edict against Origen, the edict on the Three Chapters) are not told anywhere; `council-of-constantinople-ii` starts at the council itself.
