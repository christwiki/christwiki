### B · dating · src/content/events/modern-and-global/simon-kimbangu.md
- **Text:** "the Kimbanguist account gives 12 September and 12 October [[cite:church-12]]. For Kimbangu's death Chomé gives 10 October 1951 and the Kimbanguist account 3 October 1951"
- **Problem:** The day on which Kimbangu is everywhere else said to have died, and which the Kimbanguist Church itself commemorates, is 12 October 1951; the entry (and people/simon-kimbangu.md) never gives it. The two dates credited to the Kimbanguist account look transposed: the usual Kimbanguist chronology is arrest 12 September 1921, judgment 3 October 1921 (the same day Chomé gives), death 12 October 1951. As written, the note makes the church's own account disagree with every standard reference on both the judgment and the death.
- **Should be:** Re-open the archived article; most probably "the Kimbanguist account gives 12 September for the arrest and agrees on 3 October for the judgment. For Kimbangu's death Chomé gives 10 October 1951; the Kimbanguist account, and most reference works, give 12 October 1951."
- **Evidence:** Chomé's BBOM article opened (kaowarsom.be PDF): header "Elisabethville, 10.10.1951", arrest "15 septembre 1921", "Le jugement est rendu le 3 octobre" — the entry reports Chomé correctly. The Kimbanguist page (web.archive.org) could not be opened from here (proxy failure). en.wikipedia "Simon Kimbangu" and Christian History Institute ("It happened on October 12") give death 12 October 1951 and sentence 3 October 1921.
- **Confidence:** medium (high that 12 October 1951 is the standard date and is missing; medium that the article's dates were transposed)

### C · plausibility · src/content/events/modern-and-global/dead-sea-scrolls-discovered.md
- **Text:** summary: "scholars find Hebrew manuscripts about a thousand years older than any biblical text then known"
- **Problem:** Sweeping absolute in the wiki's own voice. It echoes the IAA page's report of Sukenik's reaction, but as a statement of fact it is false: the great Greek codices with the Old Testament (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, fourth century), Greek papyri of Deuteronomy of the second century BC (P. Rylands 458) and the Hebrew Nash Papyrus (Decalogue and Shema, c. 150–100 BC, known since 1902) were all older than the medieval Masoretic codices. What is true is that the scrolls are about a thousand years older than the oldest Hebrew Bible manuscripts of any extent then known (Cairo Codex of the Prophets, Aleppo and Leningrad codices, ninth to eleventh century).
- **Should be:** "…about a thousand years older than the oldest Hebrew manuscripts of the Bible then known" (and the body's "the biblical manuscripts known before" likewise "Hebrew biblical manuscripts").
- **Evidence:** Opened deadseascrolls.org.il "Discovery and Publication" ("one thousand years older then any existing biblical text", as Sukenik's reaction) and "Introduction" ("over a thousand years older than previously identified biblical manuscripts"). Nash Papyrus, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus: own knowledge.
- **Confidence:** high

- **Confidence:** medium

### C · completeness · src/content/events/modern-and-global/mit-brennender-sorge.md
- **Text:** "The letter, addressed to the archbishops and bishops of Germany, is dated Passion Sunday, 14 March 1937 [[cite:mbs-head]]."
- **Problem:** The entry gives the date on the document and then stops; it never says how the letter reached its audience or what followed, which is what made it an event. It was written in German (not Latin), brought into Germany and printed in secret, and read from the pulpits of Catholic churches on Palm Sunday, 21 March 1937; the Gestapo then seized copies, closed the presses that had printed it, and the regime answered with renewed currency and morality trials of clergy. A reader cannot tell from the entry that the text was ever published in Germany.

- **Evidence:** Own knowledge (uncontroversial; e.g. en.wikipedia "Mit brennender Sorge"). 14 March 1937 was Passion Sunday and 21 March Palm Sunday (Easter 28 March 1937) — checked by calendar.
- **Confidence:** high
