# Sources first cited for events c. 640–1300

Reviewer batch S03. Severity: A = factual error, B = misleading, unsupported or inconsistent, C = minor imprecision or omission. See the [audit report](../README.md) for method and limits.

## Findings

### B · historical · src/content/sources/henry-iv-letters.md
- **Text:** "The letter is the king's side of the breach of 1076, written in his name and sent out with a letter in which a long list of German bishops withdrew their obedience from the pope. … Henderson's translation gives it the date 24 January 1076."
- **Problem:** Two letters of Henry to "Hildebrand" survive from 1076. The one translated by Henderson and cited here ("Hildebrand, not pope but false monk … Descend, descend") is the longer, manifesto version meant for circulation in Germany; the letter actually dispatched to Rome with the bishops' letter from Worms is a different, shorter text (Erdmann, *Die Briefe Heinrichs IV.*, MGH 1937, nos. 11 and 12). The date and place of the longer version are disputed (Worms, January, or Utrecht, Easter 1076 — i.e. after Gregory's Lenten sentence). The record presents the manifesto as the letter sent to the pope, and `events/high-and-late-middle-ages/investiture-controversy.md` builds on it ("Henry replied in a letter addressed to 'Hildebrand, at present not pope but false monk' … At his Lenten synod of 1076 Gregory answered").
- **Should be:** Say that the text cited is the longer of two versions, a circular for the German public, that a shorter letter went to Rome with the bishops' letter of 24 January, and that the date Henderson prints is the date of the Worms assembly, not a date carried by this text; soften the event's sequence accordingly.
- **Evidence:** Avalon/Henderson text opened (no dating clause in the letter itself; "Jan. 24, 1076" is Henderson's heading); encyclopedia.com "Emperor Henry IV and Pope Gregory VII" (two documents: a harsh retort for circulation in Germany as propaganda, a gentler version sent to Gregory); own knowledge of Erdmann's edition (nos. 11–12) and of Mommsen–Morrison, *Imperial Lives and Letters*.
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · dating · src/content/sources/hermann-of-reichenau-chronicle.md
- **Text:** "his death there in April, which he puts on the sixteenth day before the Kalends of May"
- **Problem:** Hermann does print "16. Kal. Maii" (16 April), so the report is accurate, but the record (and the dating note of `events/early-middle-ages/east-west-schism.md`, which repeats it) gives this as the only day and does not say that it conflicts with the accepted date of Leo IX's death, 19 April 1054 (XIII Kal. Maii), on which he is commemorated. A reader will take 16 April as the date.
- **Should be:** Add "…the sixteenth day before the Kalends of May (16 April); other sources and the Roman calendar give the thirteenth (19 April), the date generally accepted."
- **Evidence:** MGH SS 5 OCR (archive.org gri_33125006186593): Hermann a. 1054 "…illi commendans, 16. Kal. Maii"; the index of the same volume lists "13. Kal. Mai. Leonis papae noni … a. 1054". Own knowledge for 19 April.
- **Confidence:** high

### B · dating · src/content/sources/albert-of-aachen-history.md
- **Text:** "written: start: 1120, end: 1150 … display: 'Between about 1120 and 1150'"; "that of a Christian cleric of the region writing a generation later"
- **Problem:** The date is taken from A. C. Krey (1921) and is stated flatly in the front matter and in the wiki's own voice. The standard modern edition (S. B. Edgington, *Albert of Aachen: Historia Ierosolimitana*, Oxford Medieval Texts, 2007) argues that books 1–6, which contain the Rhineland massacres of 1096 and the crusade itself, were a free-standing work completed soon after 1102, and that only the later books were written over the following years (the narrative ends in 1119, not 1120). On that dating Albert wrote about the massacres within about a decade, not "a generation later", which changes how the record weighs him.
- **Should be:** `written` c. 1102–1120s (display e.g. "Books 1–6 soon after 1102; continued to c. 1120"); in the body give Krey's dating as Krey's and add Edgington's; drop or qualify "a generation later".
- **Evidence:** J. Riley-Smith's review of Edgington's edition (muse.jhu.edu/article/252984): the first half "was completed soon after 1102", against the older assumption of composition not long before 1128; Edgington's edition describes the work as covering 1095–1119.
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · dating · src/content/sources/baldric-of-dol-history.md
- **Text:** "written: start: 1108 … display: 'After 1107'"; "Krey's introduction says that his history was written after 1107"
- **Problem:** The only dating offered is Krey's of 1921. The critical edition (S. Biddlecombe, *The Historia Ierosolimitana of Baldric of Bourgueil*, 2014) dates the composition to about 1105, while Baldric was still abbot of Bourgueil, with a revision after he became archbishop of Dol in 1107. The front matter therefore dates the work after the point at which it was most probably first written.
- **Should be:** `written` c. 1105, revised after 1107; say that Krey's "after 1107" is superseded by the two-stage dating of the 2014 edition.
- **Evidence:** Cambridge University Press / Boydell description of Biddlecombe's edition ("composed around 1105 by Baldric, the abbot of Bourgueil, who later became the archbishop of Dol"); review (muse.jhu.edu/article/588951) describing a Bourgueil recension begun in 1105 and amended after 1107.
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · completeness · src/content/sources/robert-the-monk-history.md
- **Text:** "Robert did not go on the crusade."
- **Problem:** The record weighs the four versions of Urban's sermon by how close each writer stood to the event, and says of Baldric that he writes as one who saw the bishop of Le Puy come forward, but it omits that Robert himself states in his prologue (*Sermo apologeticus*) that he was present at the council of Clermont and was asked to write for that reason. A reader is left to think that Robert's version has no claim to be a hearer's.
- **Should be:** Add: "In his prologue he says that he was present at the council of Clermont; he wrote his version of the sermon a decade or more afterwards."
- **Evidence:** own knowledge of the prologue ("qui Clari Montis concilio interfui"); not re-opened in this audit.
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · completeness · src/content/sources/primary-chronicle.md
- **Text:** "The chronicle was put together by monks at Kyiv; one version carries a note by Sylvester, prior of St Michael's, dated 1116"
- **Problem:** The record never mentions the traditional attribution of the chronicle to the monk Nestor of the Kyiv Caves monastery (named in the heading of the Khlebnikov copy and assumed in most older literature), nor that this attribution is disputed. "Nestor's Chronicle" is the name under which many readers know the work; nothing in the wiki mentions Nestor (grep "Nestor" finds only Nestorius/Nestorian).
- **Should be:** Add a sentence: "It has traditionally been ascribed to Nestor, a monk of the Caves monastery, whose name stands in the heading of one late copy; the attribution is disputed, and the surviving text is at least in part the work of Sylvester (1116) and a later editor."
- **Evidence:** own knowledge; Cross and Sherbowitz-Wetzor's introduction to the translation cited discusses the Nestor question at length.
- **Confidence:** high

### C · completeness · src/content/sources/libri-carolini.md
- **Text:** "author: Written in the name of Charlemagne"; "The authors knew the Greek council only through the first Latin translation"
- **Problem:** The record treats the authorship as anonymous and collective. Since Ann Freeman's studies (from "Theodulf of Orléans and the Libri Carolini", *Speculum* 32, 1957) the treatise is generally attributed to Theodulf of Orléans, and Freeman's edition (MGH Concilia 2, Supplementum 1, 1998), which replaced Bastgen's of 1924 cited here, prints it under his name. The older attribution to Alcuin is also unmentioned. Theodulf is not named anywhere in the wiki.
- **Should be:** Add: "Modern scholarship, following Ann Freeman, attributes the drafting to Theodulf of Orléans; the standard edition is now Freeman's (MGH, 1998)."
- **Evidence:** own knowledge (Freeman 1957; MGH Conc. 2 Suppl. 1, 1998, *Opus Caroli regis contra synodum*).
- **Confidence:** high

### C · completeness · src/content/sources/ekkehard-of-aura-chronicle.md
- **Text:** "A universal chronicle continued to 1125 by a Bavarian monk, abbot of Aura"; "Ekkehardi Chronicon, in Monumenta Germaniae Historica, Scriptores 6, edited by Georg Heinrich Pertz"
- **Problem:** (1) The edition of 1844 printed the whole world chronicle as Ekkehard's. Since H. Bresslau (1896) the body of it, down to 1099, has been recognised as the work of Frutolf of Michelsberg; Ekkehard reworked and continued it in several recensions (1106–1125). The record's "continued" hints at this but never names Frutolf or says that the edition's attribution is superseded. (2) Within MGH SS 6 the chronicle was edited by Georg Waitz; Pertz was the editor of the volume.
- **Should be:** Name Frutolf as author to 1099 and Ekkehard as continuator (the entries for 1111 and 1122 cited in the wiki are in Ekkehard's part); give "ed. G. Waitz, in MGH SS 6 (ed. G. H. Pertz), 1844".
- **Evidence:** own knowledge (Schmale and Schmale-Ott, *Frutolfs und Ekkehards Chroniken*, 1972; MGH is currently re-editing the "Frutolf-Fortsetzungen").
- **Confidence:** high for (1), medium for (2)

### C · historical · src/content/sources/anglo-saxon-chronicle.md
- **Text:** "For the years around 1100 the text translated here is the continuation kept at Peterborough"
- **Problem:** The Peterborough manuscript (E) was written at Peterborough about 1121, after the fire of 1116, by copying a chronicle borrowed from elsewhere (probably Canterbury) down to 1121; only the annals for 1122–1154 are Peterborough's own continuations. The annals for 1093 and 1109 cited in the wiki are contemporary in composition, but they were not "kept at Peterborough".
- **Should be:** "…the text translated here is that of the Peterborough manuscript, which for these years copies a contemporary chronicle kept elsewhere; Peterborough's own continuation covers 1122 to 1154."
- **Evidence:** own knowledge (standard account of MS E, Bodleian Laud Misc. 636).
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · completeness · src/content/sources/privilegium-ottonianum.md
- **Text:** "The document is in two parts. … The second reserves the power of the emperor … The closing clause dates it to 13 February 962"
- **Problem:** The record gives no `holding` and does not say that the text survives not as the original but in a contemporary ceremonial copy (purple parchment, gold script) in the Vatican Apostolic Archive, nor that the second part, on papal elections, is argued by some scholars (W. Ullmann, "The Origins of the Ottonianum", *Cambridge Historical Journal* 11, 1953) to have been added in December 963 after the deposition of John XII. The date "13 February 962" is thus secure for the territorial part and debated for the election clauses, which are exactly the clauses the summary highlights.
- **Should be:** Add the holding and one sentence on the debate over the date of the election clauses.
- **Evidence:** own knowledge; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diploma_Ottonianum (opened) confirms only that "the extant document seems to be only a duplicate of the original" (Sickel 1883). I did not re-open Ullmann's article.
- **Confidence:** low

### C · historical · src/content/sources/anselm-cur-deus-homo.md
- **Text:** "English translation as printed in Sidney Norton Deane, St. Anselm: Proslogium; Monologium; … and Cur Deus Homo (Chicago: Open Court, 1903)"
- **Problem:** The translator is not named, and a reader will take it to be Deane, as for the Proslogion. The Open Court volume itself says that Deane translated only the Proslogium, Monologium and the appendix; the Cur Deus Homo is the translation of James Gardiner Vose, first published in *Bibliotheca Sacra* in 1854–1855.
- **Should be:** "Translation by James Gardiner Vose (*Bibliotheca Sacra*, 1854–55), as reprinted in S. N. Deane, St. Anselm: … (Chicago: Open Court, 1903)."
- **Evidence:** ccel.org/ccel/anselm/basic_works/basic_works.ii.html (publisher's preface): "the Cur Deus Homo was rendered by James Gardiner Vose … and published in 1854 and 1855 in the Bibliotheca Sacra".
- **Confidence:** high

### C · historical · src/content/sources/council-of-constantinople-680.md
- **Text:** "The council sat in eighteen sessions in the domed hall of the palace, with the emperor Constantine IV presiding."
- **Problem:** The emperor presided in person only at the first eleven sessions and at the eighteenth and last; sessions twelve to seventeen, including the thirteenth, whose sentence on Honorius the record singles out, were held in his absence under his representatives.
- **Should be:** "…with the emperor Constantine IV presiding at the first eleven sessions and the last."
- **Evidence:** own knowledge of the acts (Percival's summary of the sessions in NPNF 2.14; Catholic Encyclopedia, "Constantinople, Third Council of").
- **Confidence:** medium

### C · consistency · src/content/sources/innocent-iii-letters.md
- **Text:** summary: "The one cited here rebukes his legate after the sack of Constantinople"; body: "A second letter is cited in [[event:albigensian-crusade]]"
- **Problem:** The summary speaks of one cited letter; the body and the `links` describe two (12 July 1205 to Peter Capuano; March 1208 on the murder of Peter of Castelnau).
- **Should be:** Reword the summary to name both letters.
- **Evidence:** the record itself.
- **Confidence:** high

## Completeness of this stretch

Checked by `ls src/content/sources/` and by grepping `src/content` for each name below (0 files unless noted).

- **Hebrew and Arabic witnesses to the First Crusade.** The crusade is documented by ten Latin and Greek records (plus Bernold and Ekkehard); the Jewish communities' own accounts of 1096 (Solomon bar Simson, Eliezer bar Nathan, the Mainz Anonymous) appear only as a link label inside `albert-of-aachen-history`, and no Muslim account (Ibn al-Qalanisi, Ibn al-Athir) is cited at all. Grepped "Solomon bar", "Hebrew chronicle", "Ibn al-Athir". A Hebrew chronicle deserves its own record; it is the victims' own voice for an event the wiki tells.
- **An iconoclast-side or independent narrative for 726–843.** `theophanes-chronicle` itself says that "this wiki cites no narrative from the iconoclast side". The patriarch Nikephoros's *Short History* and the Life of Stephen the Younger are also iconophile, but Nikephoros is independent of Theophanes for 717–769, and the letters of Theodore the Studite are the main contemporary source for the second iconoclasm; none has a record (grep "Theodore the Studite", "Short History").
- **Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals.** `donation-of-constantine` refers to "a ninth-century collection of forged decretals" without naming it, and the only other mention in the wiki is a passing one in `council-of-toledo-589` (grep "Pseudo-Isidor", "False Decretals"); there is no record and no event. Both as a source record and as a development (c. 850) this is of first rank for the history of papal claims and canon law.
- **Waldo's own profession of faith (c. 1180).** The wiki uses a modern church web page (`waldensian-church-our-history`) as the Waldensian voice against hostile medieval reports. Waldo's profession of faith and *propositum*, made before the legate Henry of Marcy (ed. A. Dondaine, 1946), is the movement's own twelfth-century document and has no record (grep "profession of faith" finds only other professions).
- **Cathar texts.** `peter-of-les-vaux-de-cernay-historia-albigensis` is acknowledged to be "a list of charges drawn up by an enemy"; no Cathar document (the Lyon Ritual, the *Book of the Two Principles*) and no William of Puylaurens (grep "Puylaurens") is cited to balance it.
- **Adam of Bremen, *Deeds of the Bishops of Hamburg*** (c. 1075): the main source after Rimbert for the conversion of Denmark, Sweden, Norway and Iceland; absent (grep "Adam of Bremen"). Likewise **Widukind of Corvey** for Otto I (grep "Widukind").
- **Papal election decree of 1059 (*In nomine Domini*) and Peter Damian / Humbert's *Against the Simoniacs*.** The investiture entry starts in 1075; the reform papacy's founding documents have no record (grep "1059", "Peter Damian").
- **Becket's other biographers and letters.** Only Grim and Hoveden are cited; William FitzStephen (who also says he was present at the murder), Herbert of Bosham and the letters of John of Salisbury have no record (grep those names).
- **Bonaventure's *Legenda maior*, the *Legend of the Three Companions*, Celano's Second Life.** For Francis only Celano's First Life and the writings are cited (grep "Legenda maior", "Major Legend").
- **Sources for the baptism of Rus' other than the Chronicle:** the *Memory and Praise of Vladimir* attributed to the monk Jacob, which dates the baptism before the taking of Cherson, and Hilarion's *Sermon on Law and Grace* (c. 1050), the oldest Rus' text on the conversion (grep "Hilarion", "Memory and Praise").
- **Eastern Christian chronicles of the period:** Michael the Syrian and Bar Hebraeus (Syriac Orthodox), Matthew of Edessa (Armenian), the *History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria* (Coptic); none has a record (grep "Michael the Syrian", "Bar Hebraeus"), so the crusades and the Mongol period are seen only through Latin, Greek and one East Syriac text. The *Kebra Nagast* is likewise absent for Ethiopia.
- **William of Tyre** (grep: one passing mention), **Otto of Freising**, **Matthew Paris**, **Salimbene** (one mention): the chief Latin chroniclers of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries have no records; they would be needed for the events listed next.
- **Events of this stretch with no entry at all** (event-index consulted): the Quinisext council (a source record `council-in-trullo-692` exists, but no event); the later crusades, Saladin's capture of Jerusalem in 1187 and the fall of Acre in 1291 (grep "Saladin", "1187": 0 files); the military orders (grep "Templar": one mention, in an eighteenth-century entry); the Second Lateran Council (1139) and the First Council of Lyon (1245, as a council); the Peace and Truce of God; the conversion of Bohemia, Denmark and Norway; the eucharistic controversy of Berengar; Hildegard of Bingen and Joachim of Fiore; the Carthusians; the Paris condemnations of 1277; the expulsions of the Jews (England 1290). These belong to the reviewers of the event batches, but they are the reason the source list is thin where it is.

## Method note
The batch contains no `[[bible:…]]` citations (grep count 0), so no Scripture checks were needed. I read all 154 records in full and opened about 45 pages or texts: Tanner's introductions and canons for the councils of 869–870, 1123, 1179, 1215 and 1274; Avalon/Fordham texts for Clarendon, Worms, Henry IV, Urban II, the leaders' letter of 1099, Rimbert, Psellus, Hoveden, the Chronicle of Laon; vatican.va and holycouncil.org for the three modern documents; the CSMC page for the Gadla Lalibela; and archive.org OCR of Will's *Acta et scripta* (chapter numbers of Leo IX, Cerularius and Peter of Antioch), MGH SS 5, 11 and 19 (Hermann, the Stephen legends, the Pressburg and Kraków annals), MGH Epp. 7 (John VIII's letter numbers and Caspar's heading), the Annals of St Bertin, Jaffé's *Monumenta Alcuiniana*, Endlicher (Stephen's laws) and Molinier's Pelhisson. Almost every locator, number and date I tested was correct; the findings are mostly about superseded scholarship and omitted authorship debates.
Not checked: `synodikon-of-orthodoxy` (web.archive.org was unreachable, so the manuscript shelfmark, the "thirty years" and the list of patriarchs rest on the record alone); the letter numbers and dates in `nicholas-i-letters` (no OCR of MGH Epp. 6 found); section numbers in `photius-letter-to-boris`, `athanasius-the-athonite-typikon` and `tzimiskes-typikon`; the dMGH page-image links in general.
